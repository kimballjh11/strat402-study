## S01 · What Is Strategy? Porter's Generic Strategies

**Key Question:** Can Southwest move to differentiation without destroying its low-cost advantage? `[Canvas S1]`

**Reading:** Porter's Generic Strategy Framework (white paper) · **Case:** Southwest Airlines: Fixing the Revenue Problem ("In Need of a Fix")

**Slides:** S1 slides (13): 2x2, value stick, Walmart and Louis Vuitton activity systems, 'Can you compete in the middle?'

### Framework: Porter's generic strategies

Two questions produce four positions. Source of advantage: lower cost or higher willingness to pay (only two kinds exist). Scope: whole market or a narrow slice. A position requires a sacrifice; one that requires none 'is not a position; it is a wish.'

- **Cost leadership**: Lowest-cost producer serving a broad market. Room for exactly one: a shared cost advantage is no advantage and ends in a price war. Sources: scale, experience, and above all structure (business-model design). `[Porter §3]`
- **Differentiation**: Unique on dimensions buyers value, charging a premium that exceeds the cost of the uniqueness. Three engines: technology/product, process (selling certainty), people (the encounter is the product). `[Porter §4]`
- **Cost focus**: Lowest cost within a segment, not the industry (Aravind, Dollar General, Spirit, Grameen). `[Porter §5]`
- **Differentiation focus**: Uniqueness within a segment (Ferrari, In-N-Out, USAA, Patagonia). `[Porter §5]`
- **Focus is not a third kind of advantage**: It is one of the first two pursued inside a deliberately narrow segment; the focuser wins because the broad competitor is compromised by serving everybody. `[Porter §5]`
- **The value wedge**: Value created = willingness to pay minus cost. Cost leadership widens the gap from below (deleting cost faster than WTP); differentiation widens it from above (adding WTP faster than cost). `[Porter §2]`
- **Parity / proximity**: A cost leader must stay at parity or proximity on what buyers care about ('Aldi still has to sell edible groceries'); a differentiator must stay at cost proximity (Ferrari is disciplined about volume). The parity row is the one most often omitted by students. `[Porter §2, Ex.1]`
- **Stuck in the middle / U-curve**: No cost advantage, no premium, no protected segment. The U-curve puts profit at both ends of market share and a trough between. Empirically weaker than drawn; its value is locating the danger between positions. `[Porter §6, Fig.3]`
- **Operational effectiveness vs. strategy**: Moving toward the productivity frontier (quality programs, lean, systems) is operational effectiveness: necessary, imitable, and it raises everyone's absolute performance without changing relative position. Strategy is the choice of WHERE on the frontier to sit, and it always involves giving something up. `[Porter §7, Fig.4]`
- **Three tests (Exhibit 3 worksheet)**: Price test (is realized price deliberately above/below average and can you explain it?), trade-off test (name three things rivals do that you refuse to do), consistency test (do cost structure, hiring, metrics, incentives and capital allocation point the same way?). The tests are sequential. `[Porter §8, Ex.3]`

### Key terms

- **Generic strategy**: One of four positions produced by crossing source of advantage (lower cost vs. higher WTP) with scope (broad vs. narrow). `[Porter §1]`
- **Cost leadership**: Being THE lowest-cost producer in an industry while serving a broad market; there is room for exactly one. `[Porter §3]`
- **Differentiation**: Being unique along dimensions buyers value and charging a premium that exceeds the cost of the uniqueness. `[Porter §4]`
- **Focus**: Cost leadership or differentiation pursued inside a deliberately narrow segment (customer group, product line, geography, channel). Not a third kind of advantage. `[Porter §5]`
- **Stuck in the middle**: Failing to commit to a position: no cost advantage against the low-cost player, no premium against the differentiator, no protected segment against the focuser. `[Porter §6]`
- **U-curve**: Porter's 1980 picture of return vs. market share: profitable at both ends, a trough in between. `[Porter Fig.3]`
- **Productivity frontier**: The best achievable combination of cost and buyer value using current best practice at a moment in time. `[Porter §7]`
- **Operational effectiveness**: Moving toward the productivity frontier through better practice; necessary and imitable, it is not strategy. `[Porter §7]`
- **Hybrid objection**: The empirical criticism that firms like Toyota, IKEA, Zara, Amazon, Southwest and Trader Joe's run low cost and differentiation at once. `[Porter §7]`
- **Price test**: Is our realized price deliberately above or below the industry average, and can we explain the gap? Failing answer: 'We're competitively priced.' `[Porter Ex.3]`
- **Trade-off test**: Name three specific things rivals do that we deliberately refuse to do. Failing answer: a pause, or things we'd like to do but haven't funded. `[Porter Ex.3]`
- **Consistency test**: Do cost structure, hiring, metrics, incentives and capital allocation all point the same way? Failing answer: 'Our strategy is premium, and our store managers are paid on labor cost per transaction.' `[Porter Ex.3]`
- **Drift**: The real enemy: a series of individually defensible decisions that add capability without adding position. `[Porter §9]`
- **Value stick**: Willingness to pay, price, cost, willingness to sell stacked; buyer surplus, firm margin and supplier surplus sit between them. 'The goal is to drive a bigger wedge.' `[S1 slides 9]`
- **Activity system**: Tightly linked activities that reinforce one strategy (Walmart for cost; Louis Vuitton for differentiation). `[S1 slides 10-11]`

### Claims and propositions

- There are only two kinds of competitive advantage: comparable value at lower cost, or more value buyers will pay for. Brand, service, technology, culture and scale are means to one of those ends, not a third alternative. `[Porter §1]`
- The hard part of strategy is the choice; a position that requires no sacrifice is not a position, it is a wish. `[Porter §1]`
- Structure (business-model design) produces the most durable cost positions because it is hardest to copy; rivals who copy one element get nothing (Aldi, Ryanair, Nucor, Vanguard, Geico). `[Porter §3]`
- Differentiation's payoff lives in customers' heads, where it cannot be audited; 'spending customers cannot perceive is philanthropy toward suppliers.' `[Porter §4]`
- Firms rarely choose the middle; they drift into it (Sears, JCPenney 2012, Gap, Marks & Spencer). `[Porter §6]`
- Airline 'airlines within airlines' (Continental Lite, MetroJet, Song, Ted) were built in the middle and failed because the parent's cost structure, unions, hub-and-spoke and service commitments came along — evidence trade-offs are real constraints, not failures of nerve. `[Porter §6]`
- Resolution of the hybrid objection, part 1: many claimed hybrids are operational effectiveness misread as position — the firm is nearer the frontier, and that decays as best practice spreads. `[Porter §7]`
- Part 2: genuine dual advantage occurs when a business-model innovation shifts the frontier itself, and it is real while it lasts (Toyota, for decades). `[Porter §7]`
- Part 3 (most useful): apparent hybrids are usually one strategy whose trade-offs happen to please customers. Southwest is a cost leader; its choices were made for cost and some happen to please customers. Proof: it said no to what full-service carriers say yes to. `[Porter §7]`
- The practical question is never 'can a firm be both?' but 'what does this firm refuse to do?' `[Porter §7]`
- Proposition 1: advantage comes in only two forms; a strategy that does not identify which it pursues has not been made. `[Porter §9]`
- Proposition 2: scope is a genuine choice, not a description of current size. `[Porter §9]`
- Proposition 3: the enemy is almost never a competitor; it is drift. `[Porter §9]`
- All four positions usually coexist in the same industry; profitability tracks the coherence of a position more than which position was chosen. `[Porter Ex.2]`

### How it fails / misuse

- **Cost leadership: imitation**: The model is copyable (Lidl vs. Aldi; low-cost carriers after Southwest). `[Porter §3]`
- **Cost leadership: technological reset**: A new process rebases the cost curve; accumulated experience becomes an asset in the wrong technology. `[Porter §3]`
- **Cost leadership: scale ceiling**: The advantage depends on volume the market cannot supply. `[Porter §3]`
- **Cost leadership: cutting into WTP**: Removing something customers were actually buying. Measurable: Aldi has the lowest US grocery prices for decades yet ~3% of grocery spend — the market's estimate of what the deleted conveniences were worth. `[Porter §3]`
- **Differentiation: invisible spending**: Spending customers cannot perceive; a credible proxy (seal, brand, visible process, warranty) is often worth more than the underlying quality. `[Porter §4]`
- **Differentiation: the treadmill**: The premium survives only until the next product cycle. `[Porter §4]`
- **Differentiation: over-serving**: The fourth blade, the eleventh megapixel — opens the door to an attacker from below. `[Porter §4]`
- **Differentiation: premium harvest**: Quietly trimming invisible spending while keeping the price; excellent years, then hard-to-reverse erosion. `[Porter §4]`
- **Focus: segment converges**: The segment's special needs stop being special. `[Porter §5]`
- **Focus: growth forces broadening**: The most common death: the firm becomes a mediocre broad player. `[Porter §5]`
- **Focus: broad player sub-segments**: A broad rival enters the niche with adequate quality and a bigger cost base to amortize. `[Porter §5]`

### Case facts that matter

- 47 consecutive profitable years, 1973-2019, built on one aircraft type (737), point-to-point routes, fast turns, no meals, no first class, open seating and Bags Fly Free. *Why it matters:* This is the cost-leader activity system the fix puts at risk; the trade-off test is easy to pass for old Southwest. `[Southwest; Ex.1]`
- Analysts estimated free bags left more than $1B a year on the table; seat selection and extra-legroom fees generated billions for rivals. *Why it matters:* The case frames the problem as a REVENUE problem, not a cost problem — the bridge into differentiation-like monetization. `[Southwest; Ex.2]`
- The cost advantage narrowed: new labor contracts, rising unit costs, Boeing delays; Dec 2022 meltdown cost $1B+, 16,000+ cancellations, $140M federal settlement. *Why it matters:* The low-cost advantage was already eroding, which weakens the 'destroying' half of the Key Question. `[Southwest]`
- Elliott took a $1.9B (~11%) stake in June 2024; board reshaped Nov 2024. *Why it matters:* Activist pressure explains the speed and scope of the change. `[Southwest; Ex.1]`
- Plan: ~$4B cumulative incremental EBIT by 2027 plus $500M annual cost savings; ~1,750 corporate jobs cut (first mass layoff in 53 years). *Why it matters:* Management is attempting both sides of the wedge at once — the hybrid objection made live. `[Southwest; Ex.3]`
- Assigned seating fleetwide Jan 27, 2026; ~1/3 of each cabin extra-legroom; 60% of passengers paying for seat products vs. 20% baseline; Q1 2026 RASM guided +9.5%. *Why it matters:* Early evidence buyers will pay (price test moving above average on some products). `[Southwest; Ex.3]`
- 2026 guidance at least $4.00 adjusted EPS vs. $0.93 in 2025; stock +23% YTD vs. Delta +6%. *Why it matters:* Early returns favor the fix — but the case closes on three risks: reliability, holding costs flat while adding complexity, and keeping customers who chose Southwest because it was different. `[Southwest; Ex.3]`
- Kept two free carry-ons and no change fees. *Why it matters:* Remaining points of differentiation-by-refusal; check whether they are consistent with the new position. `[Southwest]`

### What a strong student brings to class

- Classify old Southwest with the three tests: price below average (deliberate), clear trade-offs (one aircraft, no assigned seats, no interlining, no meals), consistent activity system. It was a cost leader whose trade-offs happened to please customers — the paper's own example (Porter §7).
- Identify what the fix actually is: mostly monetization (unbundling bags, selling seats, basic economy segmentation) — closer to copying network-carrier practice (operational/revenue effectiveness) than to building a unique, hard-to-copy premium. The case calls it closing a 'revenue gap' vs. 'industry practice' (Southwest Ex.2).
- Run the consistency test on the new Southwest: assigned seats, premium cabins, red-eyes, partnerships and loyalty economics add complexity to a system designed for simplicity; the airline-within-airline history (Song, Ted) says carrying two strategies is how firms get built into the middle (Porter §6).
- Price test: realized fares now span basic economy to extra legroom — is Southwest deliberately above or below average? If it cannot say, it fails test 1.
- Trade-off test for the new Southwest: what does it still refuse? Change fees, a second carry-on charge, long-haul international. The list is shrinking — the warning sign of drift (Porter §9).
- Judgment: Southwest can add revenue without destroying its cost position only if the additions don't reintroduce the costs its activity system deleted (turn times, simplicity, culture). Early numbers are positive but don't yet test cost: watch unit costs, turn times and on-time performance.

### Strongest counterargument

- **Argument:** The market already rejected pure cost leadership in airlines: Southwest's margins shrank, Delta and United earn record profits on premium cabins and credit cards, and the early returns (60% paying for seats, EPS guidance 4x) prove customers will pay. Refusing to move would be choosing decline.
- **Response:** Concede the revenue gap was real, but distinguish monetizing what customers already value from becoming a differentiator. Adding fees closes a gap rivals already exploit (imitable, operational) rather than creating a premium. The question is whether the additions raise unit costs or break the activity system; the counterargument measures revenue, the Key Question is about the cost side. Hold both: take the revenue, guard turn times and simplicity, and refuse the moves (hub-and-spoke, wide-body long-haul) that would import full-service costs.

### Course map

Generic strategy is the frame around the course. Backward to industry analysis (which strategy is viable depends on Five Forces structure); downward to the value chain and cost/differentiation modules (where a position is built activity by activity); inward to organization (a strategy is a set of constraints on how the firm must be run). `[Porter §9]`

### Slide content beyond the brief

- **Value stick**: WTP > price > cost > willingness to sell; buyer surplus, firm margin, supplier surplus. 'The goal is to drive a bigger wedge some how.' `[S1 slides 9]`
- **Walmart activity system**: Low-cost operations, market position, culture/resource management and reputation for low cost linked by many small choices (deliveries several times a week, rarely use only one source, hub-and-spoke distribution, lower advertising, frugality of TMT, profit sharing). `[S1 slides 10]`

## S02 · Structured Problem Solving: Minto's Pyramid Principle

**Key Question:** What is a theory? How do I organize a compelling case? `[Canvas S2]`

**Reading:** Strategic Reasoning with Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle · **Case:** Worked examples: Granite Peak, Aldridge, Helios; assignment scenarios Tessera, Verdant, Atlas (graded with the posted Answer Key)

**Slides:** S2 slides (19): SCQA, deductive vs. inductive (Minto), logic trees (profitability, market sizing, drivers), Strategic Logic in-class exercise with key

### Framework: The two-pyramid (hourglass) model

Two pyramids joined at one point, the Answer. The top pyramid is inverted (SCQA) and funnels to the Answer; the bottom pyramid is upright and proves it. If the Answer that ends the introduction and the Answer that heads the support are not the same sentence, the argument is broken.

- **Situation**: Something the reader already knows and will not dispute: the stable current state. No argument, no tension. Establish common ground. `[Minto §2]`
- **Complication**: What has changed or gone wrong (new entrant, shifting margin, regulatory shock, activist). It makes the Situation no longer good enough and creates the need to act. `[Minto §2]`
- **Question**: The question the Complication provokes, stated explicitly and narrowly. Vague ('What should we do?') yields vague analysis; sharp ('Should we accept the national distribution deal?') disciplines everything downstream. `[Minto §2]`
- **Answer**: The single governing thought: a complete, declarative, falsifiable sentence that directly answers the Question ('Decline the deal and reinvest in DTC', not 'the distribution decision'). `[Minto §2]`
- **Deductive support**: A chain: major premise (statement about the world) → minor premise (statement about this situation) → therefore. Powerful when tight; reject any one link and the whole chain falls. `[Minto §2]`
- **Inductive support**: A group of separate, parallel arguments of the same kind that together justify one summarizing statement. More robust (lose one of three and the case is weakened, not collapsed). Must be parallel and MECE. `[Minto §2]`
- **Combined structure**: A deductive spine at the top, each premise supported by an inductive group of parallel evidence beneath it (Helios; Scenario C). `[Minto §2-3]`
- **Four quality tests**: Single governing thought; MECE groupings; valid logic (deductive chains follow, inductive groups are truly parallel); 'So what?' (every group summarizes up into the idea above). `[Minto §2]`
- **Defeat the competing answer**: State the strongest alternative fairly and dismantle it on its own terms — not a straw man (Helios dismantles 'defend hardware' inductively: three pillars, each knocked down). `[Minto §1, §3]`

### Key terms

- **Pyramid Principle**: Minto's rule that ideas are easiest to absorb as a pyramid: one governing thought on top, supported by a small number of grouped, logically ordered arguments. Lead with the conclusion, then support it. `[Minto intro]`
- **MECE**: Mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive: ideas in a group don't overlap and don't leave gaps. Coined by Minto. `[Minto intro]`
- **SCQA**: Situation, Complication, Question, Answer: the inverted top pyramid that makes the reader feel the question is the right question. `[Minto §2]`
- **Governing thought**: The Answer, functioning as the apex of the supporting pyramid. `[Minto §2]`
- **Major premise**: In a deductive chain, a statement about the world (e.g., 'All premium brands lose pricing power when sold in discount channels'). `[Minto §2]`
- **Minor premise**: In a deductive chain, a statement about the related situation (e.g., 'Cartier is a premium brand'). `[Minto §2]`
- **Topic vs. thought**: 'The pricing decision' is a topic; 'Tessera should not adopt usage-based pricing' is a thought. The Answer and every box must be a thought. `[Minto Asg §6]`
- **So-what test**: If you can read a group and still ask 'so what?', the summarizing thought is missing. `[Minto §2]`
- **Logic tree**: Break a dependent variable into drivers until you reach something you can measure and act on; every level should be MECE (children sum to the parent). `[S2 slides 11]`
- **Identity vs. hypothesis**: Sales = price × volume is an identity (always true). 'Market prices are dropping' is a hypothesis about why a term moved and must be checked against evidence. `[S2 slides 13]`
- **Key line**: The level directly under the Answer; it states the plan of the document. `[S2 slides 9]`

### Claims and propositions

- Clear writing is a by-product of clear thinking; forcing an argument into pyramid form exposes gaps in logic, thin evidence and muddled conclusions before a client sees them. `[Minto intro]`
- Senior leaders are paid to reach a defensible conclusion and make it persuasive to people with less time and more skepticism, not to gather facts. `[Minto §1]`
- Deduction: if A and B, then C — reject one link and it falls. Induction: a list of independent supports — drop one and the conclusion mostly stands. `[Minto §2]`
- Strong deductive writing anticipates and defends its weakest link (Granite Peak's major premise that its advantage is brand and relationship). `[Minto Ex.1]`
- A common inductive error is slipping a different type of claim into the group ('and the CEO is new and needs time' is about people, not combined value). `[Minto Ex.2]`
- A counterintuitive Answer raises the burden of proof, which is why a tight deductive form suits it (Helios cannibalizing hardware). `[Minto Ex.3]`
- Students are graded on the structure and rigor of the argument, not on matching a 'correct' conclusion; different Answers can earn full marks. `[Minto Asg §4; Minto Key]`
- In logic trees every join is an arithmetic operator (channels add; price × volume multiply). If you cannot name the operator, the branch is not yet a decomposition. `[S2 slides 12 notes]`
- Split a market into channels first whenever frequency of purchase and price differ substantially; a blended average hides both. `[S2 slides 12]`

### How it fails / misuse

- **Topic instead of thought**: An Answer or box that names a subject rather than asserting something. `[Minto Asg §6]`
- **Complication with no tension**: If Situation and Complication could both be true with no problem to solve, it isn't the real Complication. `[Minto Asg §6]`
- **Mixed groups**: Two types of reason in one inductive group (market + org-culture + financial). Group by type first, then order. `[Minto Asg §6]`
- **Deduction with a hidden weak link**: A chain is only as strong as its most disputable premise; name it and defend it before the reader does. `[Minto Asg §6]`
- **Ignoring the alternative**: An Answer that never engages the competing factions reads as advocacy, not analysis. `[Minto Asg §6]`
- **Evidence as decoration**: Numbers must test or prove a premise; a figure that proves nothing should be cut. `[Minto Asg §6]`
- **Not MECE / mixed logic (in-class taxonomy)**: In each answer set one option is well-structured, one is not MECE (overlapping or gappy: 'unprofitable, loses money, negative margins'), and one mixes inductive and deductive logic (a syllogism with unrelated gripes bolted on). `[S2 slides 16]`

### Case facts that matter

- Granite Peak ($40M premium outdoor brand): decline the 900-store big-box deal; DTC gross margin ~62% vs. wholesale ~31%, 68% repeat rate, ~35% price premium; contract mandates two 30%-off markdown periods. *Why it matters:* The canonical clean deductive pyramid with evidence under each premise and a named weak link. `[Minto Ex.1]`

'Expand to Europe' can earn full marks only if it confronts the capability gap and price war head-on. *Why it matters:* Shows MECE lenses (threat / capability / capital) and what an alternative answer must do. `[Minto Key B]`

A 'license the backfile to fund building' hybrid can earn full marks. *Why it matters:* Shows a fair teardown of the strongest faction on its own terms. `[Minto Key C]`

### What a strong student brings to class

- What is a theory? A theory is a causal claim that can be tested: a major premise about the world ('durable SaaS growth requires a value-aligned price metric'). Deduction applies a theory to a case; induction builds a conclusion from parallel observations. Every framework in this course (Five Forces, VRIO, the value chain) is a theory you apply deductively to a case.
- How do I organize a compelling case? Lead with the Answer as one falsifiable sentence; choose deductive when the decision turns on one causal claim, inductive when several independent considerations bear on it; put evidence under each premise; name the weakest link; dismantle the strongest competing answer.
- Pick structure by the problem: deductive for a single causal crux (Tessera), inductive for weighing independent considerations (Verdant), combined plus competing-answer for contested, high-stakes choices (Atlas).

### Strongest counterargument

- **Argument:** Leading with the answer is presumptuous; executives in principles-first cultures (France, Germany per the Culture Map) expect the reasoning before the conclusion, and a rigid pyramid can oversimplify messy problems.
- **Response:** Minto's argument is about reader capacity — readers hold few ideas at once, so give the conclusion first and the support beneath. The SCQA introduction still walks the reader to the answer. Where the audience is principles-first, keep the pyramid but spend more of the introduction on the governing premise; the structure discipline (MECE, valid logic, so-what) holds either way.

### Course map

Structured reasoning is the way to communicate every answer in the course: each case's Key Question gets an SCQA and a pyramid, and every framework (generic strategies, PEST, Five Forces, VRIO, value chain, scale, differentiation) supplies the major premise of a deductive spine. `[Course design (prompt §6)]`

### Slide content beyond the brief

- **SCQA diagram**: 'The introduction tells the reader a story about what he already knows'; the key line gives the answer to the question and indicates the plan of the document. `[S2 slides 9]`
- **Minto's deduction/induction rule of thumb**: In deduction the second point comments on the first ('Birds fly. I am a bird. Therefore I fly.'); in induction the points are separate but of the same kind ('French/German/Russian/Italian tourists... → Poland is becoming a prime attraction'). `[S2 slides 10]`
- **Logic tree: firm profitability**: Profitability = sales − costs; sales by product = unit price × unit volume; costs = production (R&D, labor, COGS, plant), distribution, SG&A (marketing, selling, overhead). Alternative cut: fixed vs. variable when the question is about operating leverage. `[S2 slides 11]`
- **Logic tree: US ice cream market**: Market = channel 1 (grocery/convenience) + channel 2 (ice cream stores/restaurants); each = average unit price × total unit volume; volume = population × % who buy × frequency × volume per purchase. `[S2 slides 12]`
- **What drives each driver?**: Sales = average price × unit volume (identity). Candidate explanations: market prices dropping, mix shift to lower-price products, total demand dropping, share declining (hypotheses to check). `[S2 slides 13]`

## S03 · Industry I: PEST and Risk in the Environment

**Key Question:** How did Khama change the PEST for De Beers? `[Canvas S3]`

**Reading:** The PEST Framework: Risk in the Environment (white paper v2) · **Case:** Botswana: Building the Foundations of a Diamond Economy

**Slides:** S3 slides (117): political risk, confiscation/expropriation/nationalization, corruption and FCPA, Gigawatt Ecuador dilemma, economic systems, GDP, PPP/Big Mac, Culture Map, technology infrastructure, IP, Botswana PEST 1966

### Framework: PEST with three disciplines

PEST works one layer above the industry: forces that act on every competitor at once, that no firm can negotiate with, and that periodically rewrite the rules. The framework's bad reputation comes from execution (a grid of true, irrelevant facts). It earns its place only through three jobs, each with a discipline: detect change (direction), locate bargaining power, and separate what is given from what can be shaped.

- **Political**: Governs political risk: regulation, tax, trade, subsidy, ownership rules, competition enforcement, stability, rule of law. Changes discontinuously, by announcement (India liberalization and Honda; US chip export controls and Nvidia; Botswana consolidating mineral rights). `[PEST §2, Ex.1]`
- **Economic**: Governs purchasing power, the cost of capital and relative prices. Matters for what the level does to what customers can afford and what the firm pays to finance itself (end of zero rates; Tesco Fresh & Easy launched into 2007-08). Moves cyclically. `[PEST §2, Ex.1]`
- **Socio-cultural**: Governs what people want, will tolerate, and who is doing the wanting. Moves slowly, then all at once as cohorts replace each other; the domain most often noticed too late (lab-grown share of US engagement rings 12% in 2019 → 61% in 2025). `[PEST §2, Ex.1]`
- **Technological (three infrastructures)**: What is possible, at what cost, by whom — assessed as three separate infrastructures (Morris & Oldroyd): physical (hardware: raw materials, manufacturing, transport, energy), information (software: communications, data storage and processing), human capital (brainware: education, corporate training, government-business-education cooperation). The shape of the profile, not one score, decides which business models are feasible; opportunities sit in the gaps (M-Pesa). `[PEST §2, Fig.1]`
- **Discipline 1: Relevance**: A factor earns a place only if a plausible change in it would change the decision in front of you. Name the decision first. Write every factor as a chain: factor → mechanism → decision affected; anything that cannot complete the chain comes off the page. `[PEST §4]`
- **Discipline 2: Direction**: A PEST run once is a photograph. Run it at two points in time and analyze the difference: the level tells you what your position is worth, only the delta tells you whether to change it. An arrow on every row beats a number on every row. `[PEST §5]`
- **Discipline 3: Bargaining power**: Every macro factor eventually answers: what happens to each party if this deal is not signed? That outside option decides the split of surplus regardless of what either side deserves. Write the grid twice, once for each side of the table. `[PEST §6]`
- **Given vs. built (shaped)**: PEST assumes the environment is exogenous. For geology, demographics and weather that's true; for much of the political, social and technological quadrants it's false, and the falsity is where the most durable advantages are built (lobbying, open standards like Tesla's charging connector, Samsung funding education). `[PEST §7, Ex.3]`
- **PEST worksheet (Exhibit 2)**: Seven columns: factor (checkable), then/now, direction (for us specifically), mechanism, decision affected, given or shaped, falsifier. `[PEST Ex.2]`

### Key terms

- **PEST**: Political, Economic, Socio-cultural, Technological: the layer above the industry — forces acting on every competitor at once. `[PEST §1]`
- **PESTEL / STEEPLE**: Variants adding Environmental, Legal, Ethical (and others). Mostly redundant; only Environmental has earned its own letter as an independent driver of cost and demand. Spend no time arguing which letter a factor belongs in. `[PEST §3]`
- **Political risk**: The threat that political decisions or events in a country will negatively affect the profitability of an investment. `[S3 slides 3]`
- **Confiscation**: The seizure of corporate assets without compensation (Mugabe seizing 5,000+ farms). `[S3 slides 8]`
- **Expropriation**: The seizure of property with compensation (Venezuela: ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips forced to abandon investments). `[S3 slides 8]`
- **Nationalization**: Government seizure of an industry, with or without compensation (Chavez nationalized cement in 2008; Morales nationalized Bolivia's three main airports in 2013). `[S3 slides 8]`
- **Creeping expropriation**: Gradual seizure through new codes, back-tax claims and administration (Mali vs. Barrick, 2024-25). `[S3 slides 9]`
- **Outside option**: What happens to a party if the deal is not signed; the better outside option takes the larger share of the surplus. `[PEST §6]`
- **Physical infrastructure (hardware)**: Raw materials, manufacturing capability/equipment, transportation networks, energy resources. `[PEST §2]`
- **Information infrastructure (software)**: Communication technologies and data storage/processing (Estonia's X-Road; UPS left-turn routing). `[PEST §2]`
- **Human capital infrastructure (brainware)**: Education, corporate training, government/business/education cooperation; slowest to build and cannot be imported at will. `[PEST §2]`
- **Trend vs. discontinuity**: A trend changes the rate at which value accrues; a discontinuity changes what creates value at all (a chemically identical lab-grown diamond dissolves the mechanism that set price). `[PEST §8]`
- **Resource curse**: The pattern in which resource wealth harms development; Botswana is the counter-example the class discusses. `[S3 slides 113]`
- **FCPA (1977)**: Illegal for US firms, citizens, their subsidiaries/agents and issuers of US securities to pay foreign officials to obtain or retain business. Anti-bribery (DOJ) and accounting provisions (SEC). Limited exception for facilitation payments for routine government action. `[S3 slides 18-19]`
- **UK Bribery Act**: Similar to but tougher than the FCPA: bars private-sector bribes and applies to both parties, not just the briber. `[S3 slides 20]`
- **Corruption**: An exchange between two partners that influences the allocation of resources and involves the use or abuse of public power for private benefit. `[S3 slides 14]`
- **Ethical relativism vs. imperialism**: 'When in Rome, do as the Romans do' vs. 'My way or the highway.' `[S3 slides 16]`
- **Purchasing power parity (PPP)**: Law of one price applied to currencies: the exchange rate should equalize the price of the same good (Big Mac index). Deviations suggest over/undervaluation; often fails in the short run and for non-traded goods. `[S3 slides 67-71]`
- **GDP vs. GNP**: GDP: value of production within a country's borders by domestic or foreign factors. GNP: final goods and services produced by domestic factors, at home or abroad. GDP = C + I + G + NX. `[S3 slides 54-56]`

### Claims and propositions

- A PEST analysis that could have been written before the decision was identified is not analysis; it is background. `[PEST §1]`
- Proposition 1: the environment is only worth analyzing against a decision, and the analysis is complete when every factor is connected to that decision by a stated mechanism. `[PEST §9]`
- Proposition 2: movement matters more than level, so run the framework comparatively — across time, and in a negotiation, across both sides of the table. `[PEST §9]`
- Proposition 3: the environment is less exogenous than the framework implies; factors a firm builds for itself are worth more than factors it adapts to, because rivals can adapt to the same conditions but cannot easily copy an environment built to suit somebody else. `[PEST §9]`
- An environment that was built can be dismantled, sometimes by a party that never appears on the industry map (the laboratory that undid the diamond cartel's scarcity). `[PEST §9]`
- PEST should be used before industry tools because it establishes the conditions under which they are valid: which structural features are load-bearing and about to move. `[PEST §9]`
- Botswana's 85/15 split moved to parity without a single expropriation because the outside options moved; the relationship survived because the party with growing power used it gradually and stayed predictable. `[PEST §6]`
- Botswana today: political and economic quadrants inverted in its favor (capital, expertise, reserves, credibility, own sales channel; counterparty loss-making and written down), but social and technological quadrants deteriorated — a stronger claim on a shrinking asset. Only the comparison shows that. `[PEST §5]`

### How it fails / misuse

- **The inventory**: True facts with no consequence, because no decision was named. Antidote: factor → mechanism → decision chain. `[PEST §8]`
- **The snapshot**: One grid describing today. Antidote: run it twice and put an arrow on every row. `[PEST §8]`
- **False precision**: Ten-year forecasts of unforecastable variables. Antidote: state directions and rates of change; if a number is unavoidable, state what would falsify it. `[PEST §8]`
- **Reading technology as a single number**: Antidote: score the three infrastructures separately and look hardest at the gaps (M-Pesa). `[PEST §8]`
- **Scanning where you already look**: The most dangerous, invisible from inside: Botswana read politics and economics brilliantly and was overtaken by a laboratory. Antidote: assign someone to the quadrant nobody in the room is qualified to assess. `[PEST §8]`
- **Confusing a trend with a discontinuity**: Antidote: ask whether a technology changes the cost of doing what we do or the reason customers pay us at all. `[PEST §8]`
- **Treating the quadrants as independent**: The lab-grown shock was technological change destroying manufactured scarcity at the same moment social change destroyed willingness to pay for scarcity; either alone was survivable. Antidote: look for pairs of factors moving in the same direction. `[PEST §8]`

### Case facts that matter

- At independence (1966) Botswana had fewer than a dozen university graduates, under 20 km of paved roads, subsistence agriculture and cattle, and depended on foreign aid. (Slides: 12 km of roads, 22 college graduates, GDP per capita $89, third-lowest in the world.) *Why it matters:* The 1966 PEST grid: weak on every quadrant, which explains the weak outside option. Note the case and slides give slightly different figures. `[Botswana; S3 slides 111]`
- Khama consolidated fragmented tribal/private mineral rights under the national government before the scale of diamond wealth was known — 'a strategic gamble rather than a response to obvious opportunity.' *Why it matters:* The central 'shaped, not given' move: it made the state the sole counterparty for every future discovery (a political act that determined tens of billions over 60 years). `[Botswana; PEST §2]`
- Khama's ethic: 'no palaces and no fancy cars'; Botswana honored the De Beers contract through fiscal strain rather than expanding output or renegotiating unilaterally. *Why it matters:* Credibility was built deliberately; Botswana as a low-risk counterparty 'looks like a fortunate national character, actually was built' (PEST Ex.3). `[Botswana; PEST Ex.3]`
- 1967 diamonds found at Orapa; 1969 Debswana formed with De Beers holding operational control, output and marketing through the cartel (CSO); 1972 Jwaneng discovered. *Why it matters:* Botswana traded sovereignty over short-term output for price stability and a route to market it could not otherwise reach. `[Botswana; Ex.1]`
- Debswana ownership: 1969 15/85 (Botswana/De Beers); 1975 25/75; 1980s 50/50; 2000s+ '?' *Why it matters:* The case's quantitative spine: equity tracked the shift in outside options. The '?' is the renegotiation question. `[Botswana Ex.2]`
- Lab-grown diamonds are chemically and visually identical, entered at large discounts; younger consumers question natural diamonds on price transparency, ethics and environment. Case chart: natural production ~1.38 → ~1.08 hundred-million carats (2019-2024) while lab-grown rises. *Why it matters:* Technological + social factors moving together — the discontinuity that weakens the asset Botswana is negotiating over. `[Botswana; Botswana figure]`
- The case's closing question: how much to use increased bargaining power for wins now vs. protecting the future relationship; pivot away from diamonds or lean in? *Why it matters:* This is where PEST's bargaining-power discipline turns into a recommendation. `[Botswana]`

### What a strong student brings to class

- Answer the Key Question with the 'given vs. built' lens: Khama did not just endure the PEST; he rewrote the political quadrant (consolidated mineral rights → single national counterparty), built the credibility that lowered political risk for De Beers (honoring contracts, no elite enrichment), and used the resulting economic quadrant (revenue, reserves, trained administrators) to move the outside options.
- Run the grid twice (1969 vs. 1980s vs. 2026) from BOTH sides of the table: in 1969 Botswana's outside option was 'leave the stones in the ground'; De Beers could source elsewhere → 85/15. By the 1980s De Beers depended on Botswana for a large share of supply → 50/50.
- For De Beers, Khama turned Botswana from a political risk into a political asset: a stable, predictable jurisdiction with high-quality deposits. That is why the equity moved and the relationship survived.
- 2026: Botswana's P and E quadrants favor it, S and T quadrants threaten the asset's value. Recommendation logic: press for gains gradually and predictably, but also use bargaining power to fund diversification and downstream capability, because the claim is getting stronger on a shrinking asset.
- Use the failure list against Botswana itself: 'scanning where you already look' — a world-class reader of politics and economics overtaken by a laboratory.

### Strongest counterargument

- **Argument:** Khama didn't change the PEST; geology did. Diamonds at Orapa and Jwaneng made Botswana valuable, and any government would have captured more over time as revenues grew. The 'credibility' story is hindsight.
- **Response:** The deposits explain the size of the prize, not its distribution. Rights were consolidated BEFORE the wealth was known, which is why every discovery became a national negotiation instead of a tribal or private windfall; and the resource-curse pattern shows deposits plus weak institutions usually produce expropriation and collapse (Venezuela output −75% under nationalism). The PEST paper lists Botswana's low-risk reputation as built, not found: consolidated rights, rejection of elite enrichment, contracts honored when breaking them would have paid.

PEST sits above the industry-level tools and comes first: Five Forces describes structure at a moment, PEST tells you which structural features are load-bearing and about to move; a cost curve assumes a technology, PEST asks how long that holds; WTP assumes customer values, PEST asks whether those values belong to a cohort being replaced. `[PEST §9]`

## S04 · Industry II: Five Forces — Where the Profit Goes

**Key Question:** How did Coke structure an industry to give itself a competitive advantage? `[Canvas S4]`

**Reading:** Where the Profit Goes (Five Forces Brief) · **Case:** The US Carbonated Drink Wars in 2026 (Cola Wars Case 2026)

**Slides:** No slides for this session (Canvas). PROVA Case Quiz 1: evaluate the cola CONCENTRATE industry with Five Forces.

### Framework: Porter's Five Forces

Before 'how do we win?' ask 'is this game worth winning?' Structure sets a ceiling on returns that varies roughly seven-fold across industries. Five parties contend for the value an industry creates; suppliers, buyers, entrants and substitutes take their share, and what remains is what rivals compete over. Industry profitability is the residual.

- **Threat of new entrants**: Not whether anyone entered, but what a determined, well-capitalized entrant would face: scale economies, capital requirements, switching costs, control of distribution, regulation, incumbency advantages money can't buy quickly (data, decades-old brand, installed base) — plus expected retaliation. Most dangerous when latent: 'High returns in an accessible industry are a countdown, not an achievement.' `[5F §3, Ex.1]`
- **Supplier power**: Strong when suppliers are more concentrated than the industry, the input is differentiated or has high switching costs, the industry is a minor customer, no substitute exists, or forward integration is credible. Labor is a supplier. Test: for every input that matters, how many qualified alternatives exist and what would it cost to qualify another? `[5F §3, Ex.1]`
- **Buyer power**: Strong when buyers are few or buy in volume, the product is undifferentiated, switching costs are low, the purchase is a large share of the buyer's costs, or backward integration is credible. Distribution channels hold buyer power independent of end customers. Power is only partly about size. `[5F §3, Ex.1]`
- **Threat of substitutes**: Performs the same function by different means, so it doesn't appear in the industry's competitive set (video calls vs. business travel; index funds vs. active; lab-grown vs. mined). Places a ceiling on price industry discipline cannot lift; wins on a trajectory, so incumbents misjudge timing. `[5F §3, Ex.1-2]`
- **Rivalry**: Most destructive with many similar competitors, slow growth, high fixed/low marginal cost, large capacity increments, commodity products, high exit barriers. The DIMENSION matters as much as intensity: price rivalry transfers value to customers; rivalry on features, brand, service can expand value. `[5F §3, Ex.1]`
- **Structure vs. position (capital allocation)**: Two axes: attractiveness of structure × strength of position. Attractive/strong: invest and defend. Attractive/weak: harvest or exit (fix fast or sell). Unattractive/strong: milk, or reshape (most commonly misread — cash reinvested into the same structure). Unattractive/weak: exit (the most expensive quadrant, where effort feels virtuous and returns nothing). `[5F §6, Fig.3]`
- **Three uses: position, exploit change, reshape**: Position where forces are weakest (segments differ); exploit change in the forces before rivals; reshape the structure (consolidation, switching costs, standards, supplier development, demand creation) — the highest-return move. Name in advance which force an initiative changes and by what mechanism; otherwise it is an operating improvement. `[5F §5]`
- **Government and complements**: Not a sixth force (Porter 2008): both operate THROUGH the five. Regulation raising entry costs strengthens barriers; interoperability mandates strengthen buyers; a complement controlled by one powerful party behaves like a powerful supplier. `[5F §7]`

### Key terms

- **Industry structure**: The configuration of the five forces that sets the ceiling on returns available to any competitor in the industry. `[5F §1]`
- **The seven-to-one ceiling**: Porter's 1992-2006 US ROIC data: average 14.9%; security brokers 40.9%, soft drinks and prepackaged software 37.6%, pharma 31.7%, airlines 5.9% — roughly seven to one, persistent for decades. `[5F §1, Fig.2]`
- **Profit as residual**: What remains after suppliers (input cost), buyers (price pressure), entrants (added capacity) and substitutes (price ceiling) take their share. `[5F §2]`
- **Latent entry**: Entry that hasn't happened yet but caps returns at the level that would attract it. `[5F §3, Ex.1]`
- **Expected retaliation**: Entrants model the incumbent's response; a credible history of defense deters entry the arithmetic alone would encourage. `[5F §3]`
- **Binding force**: One of the one or two forces that account for most of an industry's structural effect; identifying them is the objective of the analysis. `[5F §6, Ex.1]`
- **Concentrate vs. bottling**: The cola industry's vertical split: capital-light, brand-driven, ~31% EBITDA concentrate vs. capital-intensive, 5-10% operating margin bottling. `[Cola]`
- **Refranchised model**: Coke and Pepsi sell finished-goods rights to a few large bottlers while keeping national accounts, marketing and product development. `[Cola]`

### Claims and propositions

- A competent management team in a structurally poor industry will usually underperform a mediocre one in a structurally good industry; no operating discipline closes that gap. `[5F §1]`
- Five Forces is a theory of how the value an industry creates gets divided, not a market survey or list of events. `[5F §1]`
- A profitable product line with a single qualified supplier is not as profitable as it looks: the supplier holds an option on the margin. `[5F §2]`
- Proposition 1: structure sets the ceiling on returns, the ceiling varies by roughly a factor of seven, and no operating program lifts it; the decision to compete in an industry is larger than any decision made within it. `[5F §9]`
- Proposition 2: the framework earns its keep used comparatively and converted into a decision: name the one or two binding forces, state which way they're moving, specify what would have to change. `[5F §9]`
- Proposition 3: structure can be shaped, and the returns to shaping it exceed the returns to performing well within it; know which conditions were built rather than found, because anything built can be dismantled. `[5F §9]`
- A business generating strong cash in a structurally deteriorating industry is a source of funds, not a use; the burden of proof on reinvestment should be high. `[5F §6]`
- The diamond industry manufactured both scarcity and the social norm that supported its prices — the most complete example of structural engineering, and a reminder a manufactured structure can be dismantled. `[5F §5]`

### How it fails / misuse

- **Defining the industry incorrectly**: The most common and consequential error. Too broad averages away different structures; too narrow excludes substitutes and entrants by definition. Test: do firms inside the boundary face the same forces and readily serve each other's customers? Boundaries are geographic too. `[5F §4]`
- **Mistaking growth for attractiveness**: Growth is not a force; it often attracts entry and capacity faster than demand (airlines, solar modules, food delivery, streaming). Ask what growth does to each force. `[5F §4]`
- **Confusing your position with the industry's structure**: A strong competitor in a poor industry concludes the industry is fine. Structure = what returns are available; position = what share you capture. `[5F §4]`
- **Treating the analysis as a snapshot**: Run it at two points in time; a force weak today and strengthening is the one to act on. `[5F §4]`
- **Adding a sixth box**: Resist adding government or complements; identify which of the five a factor operates through. That answer is actionable; a sixth box is not. `[5F §7]`

### Case facts that matter

- Concentrate per-case economics (2025): sales $1.30, COGS $0.30 (23.1%), gross profit $1.00 (76.9%), advertising $0.28 (21.5%), G&A $0.32 (24.6%), EBITDA $0.40 (30.8%). *Why it matters:* The 'extraordinarily profitable' half of the chain; contrast with bottling at 5-10% operating margins, ROIC often below the cost of capital. `[Cola Ex.2]`
- A concentrate plant for 10% of the market costs $35-70M (<1% of a year's concentrate sales at 10% share); a bottling plant costs $300-750M for 250-500M cases; ~3 plants for 10% share = $1,575M; total entry ~$1,628M if a niche entrant can't access refranchised networks. *Why it matters:* The entry barrier isn't the concentrate plant — it's access to bottling/distribution (which Coke and Pepsi control). `[Cola Ex.3]`
- Shares 2025: Coca-Cola 46%, PepsiCo 25%, KDP 22%, private label ~3%, all others ~4% (functional soda ~1-1.5% of volume). Big three ~90%+ of volume. *Why it matters:* Concentrated rivalry; C4 ≈ 96 (three firms + private label). Rivalry has historically been on advertising/brand, not price. `[Cola Ex.4]`
- Coke raised US concentrate prices 5-7% a year 2021-2024 with volume declines under 1% a year. *Why it matters:* Pricing power evidence: buyers (bottlers, retailers) and substitutes did not cap price — yet. `[Cola]`
- Exclusive bottler territories and 10-20 year fountain exclusives (McDonald's/BK/Wendy's = Coke; Yum!/Subway = Pepsi); PepsiCo bought Pizza Hut (1977), Taco Bell (1978), KFC (1986) partly to secure fountain sales. A 20-oz cold bottle returns 5-7x the per-ounce margin of a supermarket 12-pack. *Why it matters:* How the concentrate producers structured the chain: control distribution and channels, keep the high-margin layer, push capital intensity onto bottlers. `[Cola]`
- Per-capita CSD consumption fell from 53 gallons (1998 peak) to 28 (2025); dollar sales still grew 2-4% a year on mix and price. *Why it matters:* Direction of the substitute force: water ($36B→$58B), energy ($12B→$24B), RTD coffee/tea, and functional soda (<$0.1B → $1.2B, 50%+ growth) are all moving against CSDs. `[Cola; Ex.1; Ex.5]`
- PepsiCo bought poppi for $1.95B (~20x revenue) in 2025 after its internal SoulBoost failed; Coke discussed acquisitions; PepsiCo paid $550M for 8.5% of Celsius and distribution rights. *Why it matters:* Incumbents responding to entry via niches by buying rather than building (links to Cracking Markets §7). `[Cola]`
- Regulatory front: SNAP waivers restricting soda (Nebraska May 2025; 18 states ~28% of SNAP recipients by Q1 2026; 1.5-3% volume hit in affected states), front-of-pack warnings, dye phase-outs, municipal sugar taxes; Mexico's 2014 tax cut consumption 7.6% in year one. *Why it matters:* Government operating THROUGH the forces: lowers demand and strengthens substitutes rather than acting as a sixth force. `[Cola; 5F §7]`
- Dr Pepper became the #2 single brand (tied in 2023 at 8.34% vs. Pepsi 8.31%; clear #2 by 2024) partly by riding Coke and Pepsi bottlers who carry it because it isn't a cola. *Why it matters:* Shows the distribution system as the moat and how a non-cola can use incumbents' networks. `[Cola]`

### What a strong student brings to class

- Define the industry correctly first: concentrate production (US), not 'beverages' and not bottling — they face very different forces (PROVA Quiz 1 asked about concentrate).
- Concentrate THEN: entry low threat (barrier is bottling/distribution access and brand, plus retaliation), suppliers weak (commodity inputs: sweetener, caramel, packaging), buyers (bottlers) weak because Coke/Pepsi granted exclusive territories and controlled them, substitutes weak-to-moderate, rivalry intense but on advertising not price → very high returns (soft drinks 37.6% ROIC).
- How Coke structured it: kept the capital-light, brand-heavy layer; pushed capital intensity to bottlers; used exclusive territories and refranchising to control the buyer force; locked fountain channels with long exclusives; competed with Pepsi on brand rather than price; built distribution density no entrant could replicate.
- Concentrate NOW (direction): substitutes strengthening fast (water, energy, functional soda), regulators/government acting through demand and substitutes, consumer shift; entry via niches (poppi/Olipop) succeeding because they avoid incumbents' channels; buyer power of retailers rising in take-home. Binding forces: substitutes and (through them) rivalry for the consumer occasion.
- Capital allocation: strong position in a deteriorating structure → 'milk, or reshape' — use CSD cash to reshape (functional and energy portfolios) rather than reinvest into the same structure (5F Fig.3).

### Strongest counterargument

- **Argument:** Coke's advantage is its brand, not industry structure — the brand would earn premium margins in any structure (a VRIO resource), so Five Forces overstates the role of structure.
- **Response:** Both are true and they answer different questions (structure vs. position, 5F §4). Five Forces explains why the whole concentrate layer (including Pepsi and KDP) earns ~30% while bottling earns 5-10% — the same brands, different structure. Coke's brand explains its share of the concentrate pool. And the brand's value depends on structural choices: exclusive bottlers and channel control are what keep buyers weak and entrants out.

### Course map

## S05 · Industry III: Concentration (HHI and C4) and Cracking Well-Guarded Markets

**Key Question:** How does concentration affect strategy? How do you break into concentrated markets? `[Canvas S5]`

**Reading:** Measuring Market Concentration (HHI C4 Brief) + Cracking Well-Guarded Markets, Redux · **Case:** In-class questions: crack internet search; will the DOJ allow Netflix or Paramount to buy Warner Bros; which merger makes the most sense; who should pay the most

**Slides:** No slides for this session (Canvas). The S7 deck opens with a Capital One + Discover HHI/C4 calculation.

### Framework: Concentration measures + the indirect-assault entry framework

Concentration is the most widely used proxy for rivalry, and the same arithmetic drives agency merger review. C4 is simple and blind to distribution; HHI squares shares so it registers asymmetry and is what the DOJ/FTC apply. Market definition decides the answer. Because attractive markets are defensible, entrants should attack indirectly: leverage assets they already own, reconfigure the value chain, create a niche the incumbent can't match cheaply — and combine at least two.

- **C4 (concentration ratio)**: Combined share of the four largest firms (30/25/15/10 → C4 = 80). Conventional, non-legal reading: <40 competitive/fragmented, 40-60 loose oligopoly, >60 tight oligopoly, >90 effectively monopoly. Blind to distribution inside the group and to everything outside it. `[HHI §2]`
- **HHI**: Sum of squared market shares of every firm (DOJ example 30/30/20/20 → 2,600). Runs from near 0 to 10,000 (monopoly). Squaring weights large firms disproportionately. `[HHI §3]`
- **Numbers equivalent**: 10,000 ÷ HHI = the number of equal-sized firms that would produce the same score (2,000 → 5 firms; 2,600 → ~3.8). `[HHI §3]`
- **Merger shortcut**: ΔHHI = 2 × s1 × s2, regardless of the rest of the market (12% and 8% → 192). `[HHI §3]`
- **2023 Merger Guidelines presumptions**: Concentration test: post-merger HHI > 1,800 AND ΔHHI > 100 → presumed to substantially lessen competition. Share test: merged share > 30% AND ΔHHI > 100 → same presumption even if the market isn't highly concentrated. Bands: <1,000 unconcentrated; 1,000-1,800 moderately; >1,800 highly. (2010 Guidelines used 2,500 and 200.) Presumptions are rebuttable; they shift who must prove what. `[HHI §4, Ex.3]`
- **Market definition (hypothetical monopolist / SSNIP)**: A candidate market is right if a hypothetical monopolist of it could profitably impose a small but significant non-transitory price increase; if customers would switch outside, widen it. Product and geographic dimensions. Compute concentration under at least two defensible definitions. `[HHI §5]`
- **Leverage what you already own**: Lower the cost of entry using capacity already paid for (T-Mobile fixed wireless on spare 5G: 8.5M customers; Amazon ads $68.6B on search and purchase data it already owned; Walmart Connect). `[Cracking §3]`
- **Reconfigure the chain**: Change which activities are performed so the incumbent's most valuable assets become irrelevant (SpaceX reusability ~$3,000/kg vs. Shuttle $54,000; Cost Plus Drugs removes the PBM). `[Cracking §4]`
- **Create a niche the incumbent can't match cheaply**: Valuable not for its revenue but because matching it damages something the incumbent already sells (Athletic Brewing's dedicated NA brewing; prebiotic soda). `[Cracking §5]`
- **The combination does the work**: A single move is usually answerable; successful entrants deployed at least two. Strongest pairing: reconfigured chain + niche (Hims & Hers controlled the patient relationship; Novo went from partner to adversary to distribution partner in 11 months). `[Cracking §6]`

### Key terms

- **C4 / CR4**: Sum of the four largest firms' market shares; published by the Census Bureau with CR8, CR20, CR50 and HHI. `[HHI §2]`
- **HHI**: Herfindahl-Hirschman Index: sum of squared shares of every firm; the measure the DOJ and FTC apply. `[HHI §3]`
- **ΔHHI**: Change in HHI from a merger = 2 × s1 × s2. `[HHI §3]`
- **Structural presumption**: Under Guideline 1 (2023), a merger crossing the concentration or share test is presumed unlawful; rebuttable with evidence on entry, failing firm, no real overlap, or a too-narrow market definition. `[HHI §4]`
- **Hart-Scott-Rodino threshold**: Size-of-transaction notification threshold ($133.9M for 2026, up from $126.4M); triggers filing and a waiting period regardless of concentration. `[HHI §4]`
- **Hypothetical monopolist test**: Market definition test: could a monopolist of the candidate market profitably impose a small but significant non-transitory price increase? `[HHI §5]`
- **Indirect assault**: Bryce & Dyer's finding: successful entrants don't duplicate the incumbent's model, fight for crowded distribution, or chase mainstream customers first; they attack a weak point, build advantage there, then move toward the stronghold. `[Cracking §1]`
- **The 30% penalty**: Newcomers to the most attractive markets earn returns roughly 30% below newcomers to less attractive ones: attractiveness and defensibility come from the same conditions. `[Cracking §1, §9]`
- **Fallow capacity model**: T-Mobile's term for selling home broadband over spare capacity on a network already built for wireless. `[Cracking §3]`

### Claims and propositions

- C4 cannot distinguish a 65/5/5/5 market from a 20/20/20/20 one (both 80); with eight firms the HHIs are 4,400 vs. 1,700 — 2,700 apart — and only the first is highly concentrated. `[HHI §2, Fig.1, Ex.1]`
- The arithmetic is trivial; everything contested happens one step earlier, in the choice of denominator (market definition). `[HHI §5]`
- Staples/Office Depot was blocked twice under two different market definitions; Whole Foods/Wild Oats shows that where the boundary is contested the presumption may never be reached and the case turns on customer behavior. `[HHI §5]`
- Concentration does not show entry conditions, buyer power, capacity and cost position, dynamic/potential competition, or non-price dimensions (quality, innovation, privacy, labor markets). The diamond cartel sustained near-maximum concentration and then lost value to a product made outside the market. `[HHI §6]`
- Under Armour aimed big strengths where Nike was organized to fight (footwear $1.1B, −11%); On and Hoka entered through specialty running doors the incumbent didn't defend (Hoka $2.59B +15.9%; On CHF 3.01B +30%). `[Cracking §2, Ex.2]`
- Proposition 1: attractiveness and accessibility are inversely related; the only reliable way around the 30% penalty is to enter where the incumbent has decided not to defend. `[Cracking §9]`
- Proposition 2: the entrant's advantage is a lower cost of competing rather than a larger budget. `[Cracking §9]`
- Proposition 3 (the addition): indirect entry buys time, not permanence; the head start ends when the incumbent decides the segment is worth the cannibalization, so build something that survives that decision. `[Cracking §9]`
- Ask what a competent incumbent would have to GIVE UP to respond — revenue already booked, channel conflict, cannibalized margin, organizational contradiction. That asymmetry, not secrecy, buys time. `[Cracking §8]`

### How it fails / misuse

- **Single definition reported as fact**: A concentration figure without its denominator is an assertion, not a finding. `[HHI §5]`
- **Treating concentration as competitive intensity**: Ignores contestability, buyer power, capacity, potential competition and non-price competition. `[HHI §6]`
- **Acquisition destroys what it buys**: Moving a challenger onto the incumbent's distribution removes what made it work (poppi declining in H1 2026 after the transition). `[Cracking §7]`
- **Holding company for other people's niches**: Celsius: Alani Nu +55.7% while Celsius brand −11.7%. `[Cracking §7]`
- **The niche fills faster**: Liquid Death growth 139% → 27% → ~2%; On's Americas growth decelerating. `[Cracking §7]`
- **The incumbent eventually fights**: Nike +35.4% in run specialty while On −19.7% and Hoka −7.4% in that channel. `[Cracking §7]`

### Case facts that matter

- Exhibit 2 worked calculations: 30/25/20/15/10 → C4 90, HHI 2,250, merge 20+15 → +600 → 2,850 (presumed); 35/30/20/15 → 2,750, merge 35+15 → +1,050 → 3,800 (both tests, 50% share); 20×4 + 5×4 → 1,700, merge 20+5 → +200 → 1,900 (crosses into highly concentrated); ten at 10 → 1,000, merge → +200 → 1,200 (no); 65 + 5×7 → 4,400, merge 5+5 → +50 → 4,450 (no presumption from the change); 12 and 8 → +192 (depends on level). *Why it matters:* The golden numbers every HHI calculation must reproduce. `[HHI Ex.2]`
- Real cases: Staples/Office Depot 1997 (avg ΔHHI 2,715) and 2016 (3,270 → 6,265); Sysco/US Foods 2015 (5,836, +2,800; San Diego 10,000); AT&T/T-Mobile 2011 (national >3,100, +~700); H&R Block/TaxACT 2011 (4,691, +~400); Aetna/Humana 2017; Tapestry/Capri 2024 (+1,449, 59% share, court adopted 30% threshold); Kroger/Albertsons 2024 (1,922 local markets presumptively unlawful under 2023 thresholds vs. 1,574 under 2010). *Why it matters:* Evidence that concentration arithmetic plus market definition decides real deals. `[HHI §5, Ex.5]`

### What a strong student brings to class

- How concentration affects strategy: in a concentrated market rivalry can be milder (fewer, larger players can avoid price wars) and returns higher, but (1) mergers that raise concentration face presumptions — compute ΔHHI before engaging advisers; (2) the measure is only as good as the market definition; (3) concentration invites indirect entry because incumbents defend the core.
- Crack internet search (in-class): don't attack Google where it's organized to fight (general web search on its distribution defaults). Combine moves: leverage assets you own (an installed base of devices, an AI assistant, purchase or social data), reconfigure the chain (answer engines that skip the results page), and start in a niche Google can't match without cannibalizing ads (ad-free/subscription, vertical or private search). Ask what Google must give up to respond.
- Netflix or Paramount buying Warner Bros (in-class): define the market two ways (premium SVOD subscriptions vs. all TV/video including YouTube); compute ΔHHI = 2 × s1 × s2 with the shares you assume, compare to 1,800/100 and the 30% share test, then argue rebuttal factors (entry, YouTube/TikTok as substitutes). The site's simulator lets you test assumptions — no share data for this deal is given in the materials.
- Who should pay the most: the bidder for whom WB's assets are worth most — e.g., a firm that can amortize the library over the largest subscriber base (scale logic from S8) — but the regulatory presumption lowers the expected value for a buyer whose merger raises concentration most.
- Use the 'after entry' findings: plan the second product, price the channel not only the brand if selling to an incumbent, and build something that holds when the incumbent fights back in 3-5 years.

### Strongest counterargument

- **Argument:** High concentration means low competition and should be blocked; HHI thresholds are a sound rule.
- **Response:** Concentration describes supply-side shares at one moment. A concentrated, contestable market can discipline prices better than a fragmented, protected one; buyer power, spare capacity and potential entrants (and substitutes from outside the defined market) all constrain price. That is why the presumption is rebuttable and why the definition of the market is where cases are actually won.

## S06 · Theories of the Firm: The Resource-Based View, VRIO or VRIN

**Key Question:** What is a firm? Why does it exist? Does AI offer a firm a sustainable competitive advantage? `[Canvas S6]`

**Reading:** The Resource-Based View: VRIO or VRIN (VRIO Brief) · **Case:** VRIO Assignment: Diagnosing a Firm's Competitive Position (your team: In-N-Out)

**Slides:** S6 slides (29): profit pools, how much industry matters, TCE, knowledge-based view, resource categories, VRIO table, BYU VRIO exercise

### Framework: VRIO as a sequential gate

Two firms facing the same customers, suppliers and regulation still out-earn one another because they are not resource-equivalent. The RBV rests on two assumptions: resources are heterogeneous and imperfectly mobile. VRIO asks four sequential yes/no questions about ONE resource or capability; the first 'no' stops the analysis and fixes the verdict. Its value is that it disqualifies most strengths.

- **Valuable**: Does it let the firm exploit an opportunity or neutralize a threat by raising WTP or lowering cost? Practical: if it disappeared tomorrow, would revenue fall or cost rise? `[VRIO §3]`
- **Rare**: Controlled by only a small number of competitors? If every serious rival has it, it is a requirement to compete, not a source of advantage. `[VRIO §3]`
- **Costly to imitate**: Is it costly or difficult for firms that lack it to acquire or develop it? Could a well-funded rival get it, and how long would that take? This is where most advantages die. `[VRIO §3-4]`
- **Organized**: Is the firm structured, incentivized and staffed to capture the value? Organization converts potential into captured earnings. `[VRIO §3, §5]`
- **Four barriers to imitation**: Unique history / path dependence (built under conditions that no longer exist); causal ambiguity (even insiders can't specify why it works); social complexity (rooted in relationships, trust, norms); legal and economic deterrents (patents and exclusive contracts make imitation unlawful; scale, switching costs and network effects make it uneconomic — NVIDIA's 7.5M CUDA developers). `[VRIO §4]`
- **Verdict table (brief / Canvas)**: V=No → competitive disadvantage; V yes, R no → parity ('a requirement to compete'); V, R yes, I no → temporary advantage; V, R, I yes, O no → unexploited advantage ('value left on the table'); all yes → sustained advantage. `[VRIO §5; Canvas S6 graphic]`
- **Verdict table (S6 slides version, labeled)**: The slides use an older five-row table: failure (N,N,N,N), parity (Y,N,N,N), competitive advantage (Y,Y,N,N), durable competitive advantage (Y,Y,Y,N), sustained competitive advantage (Y,Y,Y,Y), with 'Is the resource imitable?' as the column. Teach the brief's version as primary; recognize the slide labels if they appear. `[S6 slides 24, 28]`
- **Resources vs. capabilities**: Resources are stocks the firm owns or controls (tangible: cash, real estate, branches, plants, spectrum, patents; intangible: brand, reputation, culture, relationships). Capabilities are flows — what the firm is repeatedly good at, embedded in routines. Capabilities are often more defensible: you can buy a building, not fifteen years of underwriting judgment. `[VRIO §2]`
- **VRIN and the lineage**: VRIN (valuable, rare, imperfectly imitable, non-substitutable) is the earlier formulation. VRIO folds non-substitutability into imitability (a substitute is another path to imitating the benefit) and adds Organization. Still name substitutes explicitly: substitution most often ends an advantage imitation could not touch. `[VRIO §6]`

### Key terms

- **Resource-based view (RBV)**: The view that persistent differences in returns between firms facing the same environment come from differences in what they control. `[VRIO §1]`
- **Heterogeneous resources**: Firms hold different bundles of resources. `[VRIO §1]`
- **Imperfect mobility**: Bundles cannot be bought, copied or rebuilt quickly. If either assumption fails, returns converge. `[VRIO §1]`
- **Resource**: An asset the firm owns or controls; a stock (tangible or intangible). Slides: four categories — physical, financial, human, intangible. `[VRIO §2; S6 slides 12]`
- **Capability**: What a firm is repeatedly good at doing; a flow embedded in organizational routines that deploy resources. `[VRIO §2; S6 slides 11]`
- **Competitive parity**: Valuable but not rare: competence sustains parity and nothing more (regional bank branches). `[VRIO §5, §7]`
- **Temporary advantage**: Valuable and rare but not costly to imitate. `[VRIO §5]`
- **Unexploited advantage**: Valuable, rare, costly to imitate, but the firm is not organized to capture it — often the most actionable case because the constraint is internal (Intel). `[VRIO §5, §7]`
- **Sustained competitive advantage**: Passes all four tests (Visa). Substitution check keeps 'sustained' from meaning 'permanent'. `[VRIO §5, §7]`
- **Path dependence**: A resource built under conditions that no longer exist; money can buy the same category of asset today but not the same asset. `[VRIO §4]`
- **Causal ambiguity**: Even insiders can't fully specify why a capability works, so outsiders can't copy the recipe. `[VRIO §4]`
- **Social complexity**: Rooted in relationships, trust and norms among many people; can be described but not engineered on demand or bought as a unit. `[VRIO §4]`
- **Internal analysis**: Analysis of a firm's resources and capabilities to assess how effectively it can deliver the unique value it hopes to provide. `[S6 slides 7]`
- **Transaction cost economics**: Williamson (Nobel 2009): the analysis of economic governance, especially the boundaries of the firm — firms exist where organizing a transaction internally is cheaper than using the market. `[S6 slides 9]`
- **Knowledge-based view**: Firm boundaries surround critical knowledge elements (Chick-fil-A: sauce, cooking chicken, in-store operations, training inside; raising chickens, making cups/forks, making soda outside). `[S6 slides 10]`

### Claims and propositions

- The practical value of VRIO is not that it identifies strengths — every team can list strengths — but that it disqualifies most of them and forces attention onto the few assets actually carrying the position. `[VRIO §1]`
- Value and rarity are the easy tests; nearly every diagnosis turns on imitability. `[VRIO §4]`
- An asset can be expensive, hard-won and operationally excellent and still be nothing more than a requirement to compete (branch networks). `[VRIO §7]`
- Organization is the mechanism that converts potential into captured earnings; fix the fourth test and the same resources begin to earn (Intel's Q2 2026 recovery). `[VRIO §5, §7]`
- Buying a rare resource and being organized to exploit it are separate achievements, and only the second pays (Capital One / Discover). `[VRIO §7]`
- Business-unit effects explain 3-5x as much profit variance as industry effects; every study since Schmalensee finds the same ordering (e.g., McGahan & Porter 1997: industry 18.7%, corporate 4.3%, business unit 31.5%). `[S6 slides 5]`
- Most resources are parity; a page of 'yes' answers usually means the tests were not applied. `[VRIO Asg §2]`

### How it fails / misuse

- **Generic strengths**: 'Data' is not a resource; 'proprietary risk-pricing data drawn from forty years of claims' is. `[VRIO Asg §1]`
- **Conflating valuable with popular, rare with impressive**: Rubric warns against it; rarity must be judged against named competitors. `[VRIO Asg rubric (Canvas)]`
- **Not stopping at the first no**: Leave later columns blank once a test returns no; the verdict is fixed. `[VRIO Asg Ws.2]`
- **No time-to-imitate**: Imitability should name the blocking mechanism AND estimate how long a funded rival would take. `[VRIO Asg §3]`
- **Undated verdicts**: VRIO is a snapshot; note where a verdict is fragile, what would break it and how soon. `[VRIO Asg §2]`

### Case facts that matter

- Visa: four-party network, ~5B credentials, 175M+ merchant locations, 258B transactions, $40.0B net revenue (+11%), ~60% operating margin; Mastercard $32.8B at 57.6%. V/R/I/O all yes → sustained; exposure is substitution (Pix 79.8B transactions; UPI 23.66B in July 2026) and interchange regulation (June 2026 settlement). *Why it matters:* Model of a sustained advantage plus the substitution check. `[VRIO §7, Ex.1]`

### What a strong student brings to class

- What is a firm and why does it exist? TCE: firms exist where internal governance beats market transactions (boundaries of the firm). Knowledge-based view: boundaries surround critical knowledge. RBV: a firm is a bundle of heterogeneous, imperfectly mobile resources and capabilities; that bundle — not the industry — explains most of the persistent variance in returns (business unit 3-5x industry).

So generic AI is parity at best, temporary if early. AI contributes to sustained advantage only when bundled with a resource that IS rare and hard to copy (proprietary data accumulated over time — path dependence; workflow integration and switching costs; organizational routines that are socially complex) and when the firm is organized to exploit it. (Application of the VRIO brief; the brief doesn't answer this directly.)
- Also note AI as a threat to Organization: the McKinsey case — AI pressure on the base of the pyramid that appropriates the value of reputation.
- Method discipline: one resource at a time; stop at the first no; name competitors for rarity; name the barrier and time to imitate; check substitutes; date the verdict.

### Strongest counterargument

- **Argument:** VRIO is static and tautological: 'valuable' resources are identified by the fact the firm is successful, so it explains success after the fact.
- **Response:** The brief anticipates this by making each test operational (if it disappeared, would revenue fall or cost rise; name competitors for rarity; estimate time to imitate) and by dating every verdict. Used as a gate on one resource at a time, it mostly says 'no' — that is its value. The substitution check and the 'verdicts move' examples (branches, Intel) make it dynamic.

## S07 · Cost I: The Value Chain and Structural Cost Advantage

**Key Question:** How does a firm get a structural cost advantage? `[Canvas S7]`

**Reading:** The Value Chain (brief): 'A pizza that costs five dollars' · **Case:** Aldi: Focused Discounter or Broad Cost Leader? ('The Machine Behind the Lowest Price')

**Slides:** S7 deck 'Structural Cost Advantage' (25): five sources of cost advantage, value chain walk-through with Little Caesars, examples by activity. Aldi price index and cost-gap waterfall are at the start of the S8 deck (slides 2-3). PROVA Case Quiz 2: where Aldi's price gap comes from and what kind of advantage it creates.

### Framework: Porter's value chain (as an analysis, not a diagram)

A firm has no costs; activities have costs. A firm is a collection of discrete activities and wins or loses in each separately. Nine generic categories — five primary (touch the product on its way to the buyer) and four support — with margin as what's left when WTP exceeds the cost of everything to its left. The boxes are a starting point; advantage often sits between them (linkages).

- **Primary activities**: Inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing & sales, service. `[VC Ex.1]`
- **Support activities**: Procurement, technology development, human resource management, firm infrastructure — they make primary activities possible and run across all of them. `[VC Ex.1]`
- **Split until the economics differ**: Porter's nine are generic on purpose. Keep splitting while parts have different cost drivers, assets or effects on WTP ('operations' at an airline: gate turnaround vs. engine overhaul). If every activity has the same cost driver, you haven't cut finely enough. `[VC 'What Porter proposed']`
- **Linkages**: When the cost or effectiveness of one activity depends on how another is performed (more inbound inspection → less rework). The least-used part of the framework and why chains are hard to copy: a rival can observe activities but not which subsidizes which (Amazon's 2023 regionalization took $0.45+ per unit out with no single activity getting cheaper). `[VC 'What Porter proposed']`
- **Five-step analysis**: 1) Disaggregate until activities have different economics. 2) Put a cost AND an asset against every box. 3) Ask what drives cost in each box (Porter's ten drivers). 4) Work the linkages, not the boxes (including suppliers' and channels' chains). 5) Decide what to own, buy, or let someone else's brand ride on. `[VC 'How to run']`
- **Porter's ten cost drivers**: Scale, learning, capacity utilization, linkages, interrelationships across business units, vertical integration, timing of entry, discretionary policy choices, location, institutional factors (regulation, union status). `[VC 'How to run' step 3]`
- **Deleting an activity**: The most reliable cost reduction, because performing an activity more cheaply invites the rival to respond (Little Caesars: no order-taking, no drivers, no counter at the Portal). `[VC 'Little Caesars']`
- **Own one link**: Perform one activity so far beyond anyone that the industry must come to you: ASML (inbound; only EUV maker), TSMC and YKK (operations), Amazon (outbound; 6.7B US parcels), Ritz-Carlton (service). `[VC 'Own one link']`
- **Profit pools**: Total profit earned at every point along an industry's chain; profit concentration differs from revenue concentration. Value chain = efficiency instrument (your ledger); profit pool = positioning instrument (industry filings). Four steps (Gadiesh & Gilbert): define boundaries, size the pool, estimate each activity's share, reconcile. Draw revenue share on x, operating margin on y. `[VC 'Profit Pools']`
- **Five sources of cost advantage (slides)**: Lower input costs; economies of scale or scope; learning and experience; proprietary knowledge; different business models (eliminating steps or performing new activities). `[S7 slides 4, 25]`

### Key terms

- **Value chain**: The sequence of activities a firm performs to turn inputs into a product sold to a buyer; Porter's nine generic categories plus margin. `[VC; S7 slides 22]`
- **Linkage**: A dependency in which the cost or effectiveness of one activity depends on how another is performed. `[VC]`
- **Structural cost advantage**: A cost position that is a consequence of a system of choices (business-model design) rather than a cost-cutting program, which is why rivals who copy one element get nothing. `[Porter §3]`
- **Refusal-based advantage**: Cost advantage that comes from NOT performing an activity (no service departments, no advertising, no delivery); it disappears the moment breadth obliges the firm to perform it. `[Aldi]`
- **Scale/process advantage**: Cost advantage from volume (procurement leverage) or superior productivity (cut-case pallets, multi-face barcodes); tends to travel, even compound, as scope widens. `[Aldi]`
- **Profit pool**: The total profit earned at every point along an industry's value chain. `[VC (Gadiesh & Gilbert)]`
- **Proprietary knowledge**: Information that is not public and is viewed as the property of the holder (patents; hard-to-replicate processes). `[S7 slides 15-16]`
- **Lower input costs**: Via bargaining power (Walmart), cooperation with suppliers (Toyota), location (Nike), preferred access to inputs (Saudi conventional oil at $5.40 vs. Alberta oil sands at $72). `[S7 slides 19-20]`

### Claims and propositions

- A five-dollar pizza is not the result of one clever purchasing decision; it is a chain of about a dozen activities each designed to cost less — one of which, the delivery driver, was deleted. `[VC intro]`
- Little Caesars linkage: made-ahead production only works with a narrow menu; a narrow menu only works at a low price; a low price only works with high throughput per labor hour, which made-ahead production delivers. Pull one out and the others stop making sense. `[VC 'Little Caesars']`
- A value chain analysis tells you where the cost went out; it does not tell you who kept it. Ask both (Little Caesars' savings were partly captured by the franchisor: 11% fee load vs. Domino's 9.5%, $446.5k minimum buildout vs. $107.5k, mandatory Blue Line supply). `[VC 'Little Caesars', Ex.3]`
- A low-cost position that does not also deliver volume is fragile (Little Caesars ~$950k AUV vs. Domino's $1,385k — the opposite of Walmart's low cost + high throughput). `[VC 'Little Caesars']`
- The diagnostic question for Aldi: where in the value chain does the cost advantage come from, which links depend on scale, which on refusal, and which would breadth put at risk? The scaling debate turns on the composition of the gap. `[Aldi]`
- If most of Aldi's gap sits in links that scale (procurement, operations, private label), broad cost leadership is the natural continuation; if a large share sits in refusals, 'scaling up' is the slow disassembly of the machine. `[Aldi]`
- Domino's and Little Caesars have the same vertical integration with opposite governance: Domino's franchisees aren't required to buy from its supply chain and get 50% of its profit; Little Caesars mandates Blue Line. `[VC 'Little Caesars']`
- The profit pool question is not 'how do we perform this activity better?' but 'are we standing in the right part of the chain at all?' `[VC 'Profit pools']`

### How it fails / misuse

- **Not cutting finely enough**: Boxes with the same cost driver hide the analysis. `[VC]`
- **Cost without assets**: An activity cheap to run but heavy in working capital is not cheap. `[VC step 2]`
- **Assuming scale is the answer**: Go through all ten drivers; cost may be a 1998 policy choice. `[VC step 3]`
- **Local optimization**: Savings that push cost downstream into another activity or partner's budget. `[VC step 4]`
- **Accidental make-or-buy**: Activities performed only because they always have been. `[VC step 5]`
- **Ignoring who keeps the savings**: Cost removed by the chain can be captured by the franchisor, a supplier or a channel. `[VC 'Little Caesars']`
- **Drift from refusal**: For a refusal-based cost leader, every reasonable-sounding addition (delivery, digital, wider range, bigger boxes) re-adds cost; enough of them and the firm is stuck in the middle. `[Aldi; Porter §6]`

## S08 · Cost II: Economies of Scale, Learning, and Experience Curves

**Key Question:** How does a firm get a scale cost advantage? What is the real reason Tesla beats Ford and GM? `[Canvas S8]`

**Reading:** Economies of Scale: scale, learning, and experience (brief) · **Case:** Netflix: Riding the Scale Curve

**Slides:** S8 deck 'Cost Advantage' (41): Aldi anchor, sources of cost advantage, scale and scope, pizza example, Netflix Q1-Q5 model answers, scale curves, scale/learning/experience table, EVs (Tesla, Rivian, Lucid, Ford, GM)

### Framework: Scale vs. learning vs. experience

'Economies of scale' names three different mechanisms that produce near-identical cost curves. Scale is about a current RATE (output per period; a property of your configuration). Learning is about a HISTORY (cumulative output; a property of your past). The experience curve is BCG's deliberate bundle of everything that happens to total cost as volume accumulates — and a prescription to buy share.

- **Scale economies (the rate)**: Average cost vs. output per period. Build bigger, run longer, spread fixed cost over more units this quarter. Reversible: cut the rate and it's gone the same day. `[Scale 'Three mechanisms', Ex.2]`
- **Six scale mechanisms**: 1) Geometry (cost ∝ capacity^0.6 for whole plants → ~24% lower unit capital cost per doubling). 2) Indivisibility (blast furnace, sales force, codebase, Super Bowl ad — fixed across a range). 3) Specialization (Smith's pin factory: 4,800 pins/person/day vs. <20; limited by the extent of the market). 4) Bargaining (weakest: Wright's purchased material 88% and raw material 95% curves vs. labor 80%). 5) Statistics (square-root law: pooling n independent locations cuts safety stock to 1/√n). 6) Demand-side scale (network effects raise WTP, don't lower cost; don't run out at MES; markets tip). `[Scale 'Scale: economics of the rate']`
- **Minimum efficient scale (MES)**: The smallest output at which average cost is essentially minimized. Industry structure depends on MES relative to market size, and on the cost penalty below MES (shoes 0.2% of demand → fragmented; autos 21-30% → three to five producers). `[Scale 'How much scale', Ex.3]`
- **Plant vs. firm scale (the merger-memo warning)**: Real scale economies are mostly inside a plant. Firm-level economies are small (Bain: 1-2%; none of 12 industries showed moderate firm effects) and diseconomies are well documented (Zenger on R&D). `[Scale 'A warning']`
- **Learning (the history)**: Cost vs. cumulative output. Wright 1936: 80% curve for direct LABOR only (exponent 0.322), cumulative-average not unit; the whole airplane curve flattens from 83% to 90%. Largely irreversible, though it decays. `[Scale 'Learning']`
- **Learning-curve arithmetic**: Y = a·X^−b; progress ratio s = 2^−b, b = −log₂ s; cut per doubling = 1 − s; doublings d = log₂(X₂/X₁), cost ratio = s^d; fit s = (Y₂/Y₁)^(1/d); doublings to close a gap d = ln(C_follower/C_leader) ÷ (−ln s), then required volume = today's × 2^d. Ask which convention 'learning rate' means (s or 1−s). `[Scale App.A]`
- **Experience curve (BCG doctrine)**: All costs 'of every kind' in constant dollars vs. accumulated volume, 20-30% per doubling, 'without limit'. Prescription: 'Market share must be maintained at all costs as long as growth rate exceeds the anticipated rate of return'; price new products 'as low as necessary to dominate'. `[Scale 'Experience']`
- **Why they can't be separated**: Pure geometry (24% per doubling of plant capacity) sits inside BCG's 20-30% band; in a growing industry plant capacity and cumulative volume double together, so no cost series distinguishes them. When separated (Thompson's Liberty ships), learning's share was small (~70% capital deepening; ~5% residual). `[Scale 'Why it is difficult']`
- **The three diagnostic questions**: 1) Is cost falling because of how much I make this period or how much I've ever made? 2) Would it survive a year at half the volume? 3) Can my competitor read about it (does learning leak)? `[Scale 'Back to the shipyard']`

### Key terms

- **Economies of scale**: Brief: a relationship between average cost and output per period. Slides: a reduction in cost per unit due to increased efficiency as the number of goods produced increases (technical, managerial, financial/marketing, network economies). `[Scale; S8 slides 6]`
- **Economies of scope**: A reduction in average total cost from increasing the number of DIFFERENT goods produced — from sharing R&D, marketing/brand, distribution, retail relationships (P&G-style), or technology, customer accounts, fulfilment, last mile, Prime, forecasting (Amazon). `[S8 slides 10-13]`
- **Learning curve**: Brief: cost vs. cumulative output (Wright: direct labor). Slides: labor costs per unit decrease with increases in volume due to learning. `[Scale; S8 slides 33]`
- **Experience curve**: BCG's representation of the relationship between cumulative volume and total product cost; bundles scale, learning and technical change. `[Scale; S8 slides 33]`
- **Progress ratio (s)**: Cost at 2X ÷ cost at X; an 80% curve leaves cost at 80% after each doubling (a 20% cut). `[Scale App.A]`
- **Minimum efficient scale**: Smallest output at which average cost is essentially minimized. `[Scale]`
- **Square-root law**: Consolidating n independent locations leaves safety stock at 1/√n (sixteen into one → −75%); collapses if demands move together. `[Scale App.A]`
- **Six-tenths rule**: Whole-plant capital cost rises with capacity^0.6 (Chilton 1950): doubling capacity costs 1.52x, so unit capital cost falls to 0.758x (−24.2%). `[Scale App.A, Ex.A2]`
- **Diseconomies of scale**: Williamson's four: atmospheric consequences, bureaucratic insularity, incentive limits of employment, communication distortion. Brooks: n(n−1)/2 channels (50 people → 1,225). `[Scale 'Diseconomies']`
- **Negative learning**: Costs rising with experience: French nuclear construction costs rose (×3 Grubler; ~60% Komanoff) as reactor size grew. `[Scale 'Negative learning']`
- **Learning leaks / spillovers**: Rivals pick up part of your learning (nuclear ~1/3; shipyards spillovers may exceed own learning) — it may be an industry public good you're paying for. `[Scale 'Learning leaks']`

### Claims and propositions

- Scale is about a current rate; learning is about a history. `[Scale 'Three mechanisms']`
- Most firms discover which one they had involuntarily, in a recession when demand drops. `[Scale box]`
- What determines industry structure is not whether scale economies exist but the ratio of MES to market size. `[Scale 'How much scale']`
- Entry to the leading edge of chips rose ~14x in twenty years ($2bn → $28bn per fab) and the number of firms who can buy one fell from 18 to 3 — a scale story. `[Scale Ex.4]`
- The 80% curve is not a law: Dutton & Thomas found progress ratios from 55% to 108% across 100+ studies. `[Scale Ex.5]`
- Learning decays: Bongers finds only ~7% of Super Hornet experience retained year to year; a paused program does not resume where it stopped. `[Scale 'Learning decays']`
- Texas Instruments priced the TI-99/4A down to $99 (and $49) on an experience curve it did not own; Commodore, vertically integrated into its own chips (MOS Technology), sat on a lower curve. TI lost $145M in 1983 and exited. `[Scale 'Where the doctrine goes wrong']`
- Ghemawat's conditions: wrong technology kills you regardless of volume; slow-doubling industries can't pay; forcing output can raise cost (TriStar); substitution destroys the accumulated base (cellophane). Du Pont TiO2: mechanics worked, demand didn't. `[Scale 'Ghemawat']`
- Rigidity: Ford cut Model T cost ~3/4 and took share 10% → 55%, then needed most of a year (~$200M) to retool for the Model A, handing the market to GM. `[Scale 'rigid']`
- Learning lodges in people more than institutions (surgeon volume OR 0.29 vs. hospital 0.35); corporate size is not learning — hospital acquisitions made patient experience worse (−0.17 SD). `[Scale 'A version with no factory']`
- Blockbuster had size without scale economies; Netflix had scale economies before it had size. `[S8 slides 15 notes]`
- Scale economies tell you average cost falls; they don't tell you who captures the surplus — rented inputs from concentrated suppliers leak the value upstream (Starz $30M → $300M). `[S8 slides 21; Netflix]`

### How it fails / misuse

- **Calling size 'scale'**: Blockbuster: 9,000 stores, flat average cost — MES was one store. `[Netflix; S8 slides 15]`
- **Firm-level scale claims in merger memos**: Evidence for firm-level economies is thin; diseconomies are documented. `[Scale 'A warning']`
- **Experience doctrine as pricing rule**: Pricing ahead of cost on a curve you don't own (TI). `[Scale]`
- **Ignoring Ghemawat's conditions**: Wrong technology, slow doubling, forced output, substitution. `[Scale]`
- **Ignoring leakage and decay**: Your accumulated volume advantage is smaller than the model says. `[Scale]`
- **Diseconomies past MES**: Bureaucracy, communication overload, weaker incentives. `[Scale]`

### Case facts that matter

- Blockbuster: 9,000+ stores and ~$6B revenue at the 2004 peak; each store replicated the full cost structure (lease, labor, local inventory); late fees ~$800M (~15% of revenue) in 2000; ~$1B spin-off debt; Total Access losing up to $400M/yr; bankrupt Sept 2010. *Why it matters:* Size without scale economies: average cost per rental about the same for 9,000 stores as for 900. `[Netflix; Ex.2]`
- DVD-by-mail: one national inventory, ~60 distribution centers, 95% next-day by 2007, Cinematch steering to the back catalog (>half of queue adds from recommendations); subscribers 1M (2002) → 5M (2006) → ~10M (2008). *Why it matters:* Fixed costs (website, recommendation system, DC network) spread over every subscriber; demand pooling raised utilization — a downward-sloping curve with a postage floor. `[Netflix]`
- Streaming: content and platform become fixed; AC = F/N + c with c a few cents — cost per subscriber halves with every doubling of N. At F = $17B: 10M subs → $1,700/yr ($142/mo); 50M → $340 ($28); 100M → $170 ($14); 200M → $85 ($7.10); 300M → $57 ($4.70). Breakeven on content alone at ~$12 ARPU needs ~118M subscribers. *Why it matters:* The steepness is the whole story: small differences in N produce enormous differences in unit cost, and the leader's spend raises everyone's MES. `[Netflix Ex.5; S8 slides 17-18]`
- Licensing leaked: Starz ~$30M/yr (2008) → Netflix offered $300M+ (2011) and Starz walked; 2019 reclamations (Disney; The Office $500M/5 yrs; Friends $425M/5 yrs). *Why it matters:* Concentrated suppliers with hold-up power captured the value of Netflix's scale; originals = vertical integration into the input (cost certainty, global perpetual rights, exclusivity). `[Netflix Ex.3; S8 slides 21]`
- Content spend: Netflix $1.8B (2012) → $12.0B (2018) → $17.5B (2021) → ~$18B planned (2025) vs. HBO ~$2-2.5B. Per subscriber: $17B/200M = $85 vs. $2.5B/40M = $63; at 300M, ~$57. *Why it matters:* Seven times the spend, about the same unit cost; HBO couldn't match because of distribution (MVPDs), the parent's profit pool, and an organization calibrated to a linear schedule. `[Netflix Ex.4; S8 slides 23]`
- Operating margin 1.4% (2012) → 4.3% (2016) → 12.9% (2019) → 20.6% (2023) → ~29.5% (2025); FCF bottomed at −$3.3B (2019), +$6.9B (2023-24); revenue ~$45B (2025). *Why it matters:* Fixed-cost leverage: cost share of revenue falls, margin rises as revenue grows against a fixed content base. `[Netflix Ex.6; S8 slides 28]`
- Q3 2025: Netflix quarterly streaming operating profit $2.55B vs. Disney+/Hulu $352M, HBO Max $345M, Paramount+ $340M, Peacock −$217M. *Why it matters:* Rivals sit far up the average-cost curve. `[Netflix Ex.7]`
- Professor's scale curves: cost per subscriber per month 2006-2013 fit y = 181.61x^−0.297 (R² 0.95) → ~18% per doubling ('scale curve'); 2011-2025 ~4% ('experience curve'). *Why it matters:* The answer to the case's Assignment 1 — the curve flattens as Netflix reaches the bottom. `[S8 slides 39-40]`
- EVs (2025): Tesla 9.0M cumulative, learning rate 7.4%, $46,978 cost per vehicle; Rivian 174k, 18.5%, $123,995, needs 4.7 doublings = 27x its output to date; Lucid 43k, 16.4%, $146,300, 6.4 doublings = 82x. Ford Model e: $4.8B EBIT loss on $6.7B revenue → $32k-57k loss per vehicle; GM est. $12k-18k loss. Tesla takes nearly half of US EV sales. *Why it matters:* The real reason Tesla beats Ford and GM: cumulative EV volume on a NEW cost curve (battery, software, electrical architecture). Incumbents' scale sits in stamping, assembly and distribution; they have volume of the wrong kind. `[S8 slides 31-32 + notes]`

### What a strong student brings to class

- How a firm gets a scale cost advantage: locate the fixed costs, find the unit at which cost is incurred (store vs. nation), measure the slope, and ask who appropriates the gains (S8 synthesis). Netflix moved costs from variable to fixed with each pivot, making each curve steeper, and then pushed MES beyond rivals by raising the content ante.
- Test it with the brief's diagnostic: Netflix's advantage is scale (rate of subscribers), not learning — halve subscribers and cost per subscriber doubles. And it's supply-side, not a network effect: one subscriber's utility doesn't rise because another joins, so it's contestable by a rival willing to finance years of losses (as Netflix proved).
- Tesla vs. Ford/GM: not 'better management' — it's which curve. EV cost falls with cumulative EV output (battery packs, software, architecture). Tesla's 9M cumulative units put it far down that curve; Rivian and Lucid learn faster (18.5%, 16.4%) but each doubling needs twice the volume (27x, 82x output to date — past a century at 2025 rates). Ford/GM have scale in the old curve. Two defensible escapes: stop competing on a dimension decided by cumulative volume, or reset the curve with a different process (S8 slide 31 notes).
- Durability (Q5): for — rising fixed-cost ante (sports rights), new layers on the same base (ads, games, live), consolidation, suppliers need Netflix's checkbook again. Against — content is fixed but not sunk-once (paid every year), talent and rights holders capture rents, international ARPU ~1/3 of US flattens the revenue side, and YouTube/TikTok shift the basis of competition.

### Strongest counterargument

- **Argument:** Netflix's advantage is differentiation (originals, brand, recommendation engine), not cost; Squid Game and Stranger Things are why people subscribe.
- **Response:** Originals are the differentiation, but the reason Netflix can afford a library rivals can't is cost per subscriber: the same $17B costs it ~$57 per subscriber per year and costs a 41M-subscriber rival seven times that. Exclusivity and recommendations sit on top of a scale cost advantage (S8 slides 21, 23). Both sides of the wedge are moving — a preview of dual advantage (S11).

## S09 · Differentiation I: Product Differentiation

**Key Question:** Is what the company sees as value-add recognized and rewarded by customers? `[Canvas S9]`

**Reading:** Differentiation: what it is, where WTP comes from, how to tell whether you have it, and how fast it decays (brief) · **Case:** Chipotle: Out-Cooking the Competition

**Slides:** S9 deck 'Differentiation Advantage' (40; title slide reads STRAT 392): strategy canvas and four actions (Cirque, Wii, Airbnb), jobs to be done, consumption chain, segmentation, NPS, consumer surplus, price discrimination, Ferrari profit per car

### Framework: Differentiation on the value stick

Being different is not differentiation. Differentiation is the ability to raise what a buyer will pay by more than it costs to create the reason they pay. It counts only if the buyer both perceives it and pays (or is willing to pay) for it. The definitive test is a realized price premium, and it has a signature in the accounts.

- **The value stick**: Four levels: supplier opportunity cost (what a supplier would accept), cost, price, willingness to pay. Value created = WTP − supplier opportunity cost. Price splits it: customer surplus (WTP − price), firm margin (price − cost), supplier surplus (cost − supplier opportunity cost). Differentiation moves the top; cost leadership moves the bottom; only the first creates the option to charge more. `[Diff §1, Fig.1]`
- **Six sources of WTP**: Performance (TSMC node lead), risk reduction (Old Dominion: 42% more per cwt, 99% on time, 0.1% claims), time and convenience (+1.45% online sales per business day cut), switching and integration (NVIDIA 7.5M developers), identity and signal (Hermès 41.0% vs. Kering 12.8% operating margin), trust in a market the buyer can't verify (auto repair). `[Diff §2]`
- **Search/experience/credence goods**: Normal goods: quality knowable before/after purchase; experience goods: only after; credence goods: never known (medical, legal, repair). For credence goods differentiation can't be demonstrated, only signaled. `[Diff §2]`
- **Six tests for a premium**: Price (realized vs. a matched competitor, not list); volume at parity; elasticity (price rise accepted at the shelf, not in a survey); retention (tracks satisfaction, not switching cost); mix (attach rate of higher-margin options); where it lands (gain in price = differentiation; gain entirely in cost = an operating improvement). `[Diff §3]`
- **Differentiation decays**: US non-financials 1970-2024: average annual ROIC fade 0.21 (consumer staples 0.10, health care 0.18, IT 0.20, energy 0.29, utilities 0.30); market-implied advantage periods 5-20 years; Wiggins & Ruefli: superior performance rarely persists. Lululemon gross margin 59.2 → 56.6 → 54.2%. `[Diff §5, Fig.3]`
- **Five ways it fails**: The buyer didn't want the attribute (JCPenney fair pricing); copied before it could pay (Casper, ~175 bed-in-a-box brands); matching a rival broke the profit formula (Blockbuster ending late fees); the feature was real and nobody would pay (Fire Phone); the premium didn't survive normalization (Peloton). `[Diff §6, Ex.2]`
- **Four durability mechanisms**: Path dependence, causal ambiguity, social complexity, credible commitment (a rival could match the promise but won't surrender the option needed to make it believable — guarantees, irrevocable governance, posted pricing rules). A differentiator resting on none of the four is a routine, and routines travel. `[Diff §7]`
- **Six-step diagnostic**: 1) Name the buyer and the alternative. 2) State the premium as a realized number. 3) Name which of the six sources produces it and whether the buyer can verify it or must be given a signal. 4) Name which durability mechanism prevents imitation. 5) Check where the gain lands. 6) Ask what would make the premium gone in five years; falling gross margin at constant mix is usually the first signal. `[Diff §8]`
- **Tools to find differentiation (slides)**: Strategy canvas and the four actions (eliminate, reduce, raise, create); jobs to be done; mapping the consumption chain; customer segmentation. `[S9 slides 4-11]`

### Key terms

- **Differentiation**: Brief: the ability to raise what a buyer will pay by more than it costs to create the reason. Slides: offering value not available elsewhere or that others don't do as well; providing unique value that commands a premium price. `[Diff §1; S9 slides 2, 15]`
- **Willingness to pay (WTP)**: The most a buyer would pay; the top of the value stick. `[Diff §1]`
- **Supplier opportunity cost**: What a supplier would accept; the bottom of the value stick. `[Diff Fig.1]`
- **Signature of a premium**: The firm holds a share of profit its share of volume doesn't explain, or raises price without losing volume (Apple ~21% of units, ~49% of revenue; Ferrari shipments −3.7%, car and parts revenue +8.0%). `[Diff §3, Fig.2]`
- **Credence good**: A good whose quality is never known even after use; differentiation must be signaled. `[Diff §2]`
- **Information rent**: Part of brand equity that is a rent on buyer ignorance (pharmacists buy national-brand remedies 9% vs. 26% for average consumers); erodes as information improves. `[Diff §4]`
- **Credible commitment**: A promise structured so the firm can't walk it back; competitors can say the words but can't sign the document. `[Diff §7]`
- **Strategy canvas / value curve**: Plot of offering levels across competing factors; blue-ocean players diverge (raise/create some factors, eliminate/reduce others) and show peaks and valleys, not a flat line. `[S9 slides 5-6]`
- **Four actions framework**: Eliminate (factors the industry takes for granted), reduce (well below standard), raise (well above standard), create (never offered). `[S9 slides 8]`
- **Jobs to be done**: What the customer 'hires' a product to do; differentiation sources: does a job better, does more jobs, does a unique job, better quality/reliability, more convenient, brand/image. `[S9 slides 12-15]`
- **Consumption chain**: Every step of the customer's experience: awareness (Listerine), finding (Coke), selecting (Uber), ordering/purchasing (Dollar Shave Club), delivery/installation and payment (RC Willey), storage/moving (Little Giant ladders), help in use (Butterball), returns/exchange (Nordstrom/Zappos), repair/disposal (Patagonia Worn Wear). `[S9 slides 18-29]`
- **Customer segmentation**: Grouping customers by similar needs — by product attributes, customer demographics, or job to be done (Christensen & Raynor). `[S9 slides 30-32]`
- **Net Promoter Score**: 'How likely are you to recommend us?' 9-10 promoters, 7-8 passive, 1-6 detractors; NPS = % promoters − % detractors. `[S9 slides 34]`
- **Consumer surplus**: Value customers obtain by buying for less than they'd be willing to pay; the area under the demand curve above price. `[S9 slides 36-37]`
- **Price discrimination**: Charging higher prices to consumers with higher WTP: 1st degree (each customer's reservation price — auctions), 2nd degree (by quantity — bulk discounts), 3rd degree (by group — student/senior, business/economy). `[S9 slides 38]`

### Claims and propositions

- A firm can be unmistakably different and earn nothing — the ordinary fate of most features — and nearly indistinguishable yet command a premium. `[Diff §1]`
- Any WTP figure quoted without naming a product and a price point is close to meaningless: the same fair-trade label held an 8% price rise on one coffee (elasticity 0.28) and lost ~30% volume on another for 9% (−3.32). `[Diff §4]`
- Survey-based WTP has no budget constraint; act on field experiments and purchase records. `[Diff §4, Ex.3]`
- Durability does not come from the attribute; it comes from why a competitor cannot supply the same attribute at the same cost. `[Diff §7]`
- If profits improved without any price moving, the achievement was in cost, and cost achievements diffuse. `[Diff §8]`
- Spending customers cannot perceive is philanthropy toward suppliers; a credible proxy is often worth more than the underlying quality (and the premium harvest — trim invisible spending, keep the price — works until it doesn't). `[Porter §4]`
- Chipotle's rivals are a market test: every rival invests in the freshness customers can SEE; none matches the sourcing standards customers must take on faith. `[Chipotle Ex.5]`

### How it fails / misuse

- **The buyer did not want the attribute**: JCPenney 2012: net sales $17,260M → $12,985M, comps −25.2%, loss $985M; 'did not resonate with our customers.' `[Diff §6]`
- **Copied before it could pay**: Casper: IPO $12 (Feb 2020), taken private at $6.90 (Jan 2022); the barrier to copying was a website. `[Diff §6]`
- **Matching a rival broke the profit formula**: Blockbuster ended late fees (worth $250-300M operating income) in 2005, lost $588M, reinstated in 2010, bankrupt 206 days later. `[Diff §6]`
- **Nobody would pay for the feature**: Fire Phone $199 → $0.99 in six weeks; $170M charge. `[Diff §6]`
- **Premium didn't survive normalization**: Peloton bike $2,245 → $1,495 in under a year; revenue −39% from peak; first profit from subscriptions at 71.1% gross margin. `[Diff §6]`
- **Attribute any landlord can copy (B2B)**: WeWork: $47B valuation, Chapter 11 in 2023. `[Diff §6]`

### Case facts that matter

- 2025: revenue $11.92B, 4,056 restaurants, net income $1.54B, restaurant-level margin 25.4% (from 26.7%), operating margin 16.2%; comparable sales −1.7% (first decline since the crisis years); management guided flat comps for 2026 while opening 350+ restaurants a year. *Why it matters:* The squeeze: price lever spent after ~20% cumulative increases 2021-24. `[Chipotle; Ex.1]`
- Food, beverage and packaging 29.6% of revenue (~$3.5B); each 100 bps ≈ $120M of operating profit — close to a tenth of earnings. *Why it matters:* The arithmetic that makes cutting tempting. `[Chipotle; Ex.6]`
- Differentiation choices: responsibly raised meats (pulled carnitas from a third of stores in 2015 over an audit), 53 whole ingredients, fresh daily prep in view, short menu/assembly line, no franchising, digital second line and Chipotlanes (digital 36.7% of food & beverage revenue). *Why it matters:* Some are visible at purchase (prep, line, portions); some must be taken on faith (sourcing). `[Chipotle; Ex.2]`
- WTP evidence: ~20% cumulative price increases 2021-24 while transactions still grew; AUV ~$3.1-3.2M (roughly double typical franchised QSR); restaurant margins 25-27%; Papa John's ~5% operating margin despite also claiming ingredient superiority. *Why it matters:* The cleanest test of WTP (price rose, volume held) — and the comparison set shows a claim alone earns nothing. `[Chipotle; Ex.3, Ex.4]`
- 2015-16 food-safety crisis: comps −20.4%, net income $476M → $23M, restaurant margin 26.1% → 12.8%. *Why it matters:* A company that charges for superior quality has made a promise every daily operation can break (trust as a WTP source cuts both ways). `[Chipotle; Ex.1]`
- 2024 'skimpflation' backlash: customers filmed bowls; management recommitted to generous portions at a cost in margin. *Why it matters:* Customers police the visible offer instantly — they watch the bowl, not the chicken certification. `[Chipotle]`
- Rivals (Qdoba ~750 franchised units with free queso/guac; Cafe Rio and Costa Vida tortillas pressed in view with commissary prep and conventional sourcing; Moe's; Cava) signal freshness while spending less. *Why it matters:* Evidence that the visible half of the bundle may carry most of the WTP. `[Chipotle Ex.5]`
- Boatwright's options: relax sourcing ($150-300M, not visible, brand-identity risk); commissary prep ($100-200M, mostly not visible); trim portions ~5% (~$175M, visible immediately, highest detection risk); substitute ingredients ($50-150M, rarely visible); cut elsewhere ($50-100M); reframe standards as value (negative savings; makes the invisible visible). *Why it matters:* The decision expressed as visibility × risk. `[Chipotle Ex.6]`

### What a strong student brings to class

- Apply the brief's diagnostic: buyer = fast-casual diner choosing Chipotle vs. Qdoba/Cava; premium = realized menu price above rivals, proven by 2021-24 price increases with growing traffic; sources = performance (taste/freshness) and trust (food integrity — partly credence, so it must be signaled); durability = social complexity and credible commitment (company-owned execution, public sourcing standards).
- Answer the Key Question by splitting the bundle: the VISIBLE value-adds (fresh prep, the line, portion size, speed) are clearly recognized and rewarded — customers police them. The INVISIBLE ones (responsibly raised protein, some of the 53-ingredient purity) are recognized only as brand belief; the rivals' success with conventional sourcing suggests they carry less WTP directly, but they underwrite the trust claim and the crisis showed how much that trust is worth.
- Recommendation logic: don't trim what customers can see (portions — highest detection risk); be wary of 'premium harvest' on invisible sourcing (Porter §4: works beautifully until it doesn't); the stronger play is to make the invisible visible (reframe standards as value) or cut costs elsewhere — and acknowledge WTP has a ceiling (2025 counter-evidence).
- Connect to the value stick: cutting invisible cost moves the bottom (a cost move); if it erodes the trust that holds WTP up, it lowers the top more than it lowers the bottom.

### Strongest counterargument

- **Argument:** Customers can't see sourcing, rivals prove they don't pay for it, and each 100 bps is $120M. Cutting invisible costs is just good management — the premium lives in perception, which would survive intact.
- **Response:** Concede the visible/invisible split is real, then point to the mechanism: Chipotle's premium rests on a credible commitment to food integrity; for a credence-like attribute the signal is the commitment itself. The 2015-16 crisis (net income $476M → $23M) shows the downside when the claim is breached, and audits, employees and media make quiet cuts discoverable. 'Premium harvest' produces several excellent years and then erosion that is very hard to reverse (Porter §4). Test before you cut: run the six premium tests and watch gross margin at constant mix.

## S10 · Differentiation II: Beyond-Product (Alternative) Differentiation

**Key Question:** Is beyond-product differentiation better or worse than product differentiation? `[Canvas S10]`

**Reading:** Differentiation Brief (re-read) — no new brief for this session · **Case:** A More Company: Valuation or Value Creation

**Slides:** No slides for this session (Canvas). The last S9 slide shows the Clegg Auto / A More Company logo.

### Framework: Beyond-product differentiation, judged with the Differentiation Brief

Product differentiation raises WTP through the offering itself (performance, features). Beyond-product differentiation raises WTP — or lowers cost — through what surrounds the product: the people who deliver it, the trust they earn, the ownership and governance that make promises credible, the community and movement around the firm. Judge it with the same tests: does the buyer perceive and pay, which source of WTP is it, which durability mechanism protects it, and where does the gain land?

- **People as the differentiation engine**: Porter's third engine: 'people, where the encounter is the product' (Ritz-Carlton, Chick-fil-A, USAA, Nordstrom). Clegg's theory: a business that helps a person progress toward what they want produces an employee who cares, who produces work customers value. `[Porter §4; More Co]`
- **Trust in a market the buyer cannot verify**: Auto repair is a trust/credence business: 'customers cannot verify the work and must believe the person recommending it.' The brief's own example: 63% of US drivers don't trust repair shops, 64% say they've found one they do. `[More Co; Diff §2]`
- **Credible commitment through structure**: A perpetual purpose employee ownership trust: the company can never be sold; liquidation goes to charity; five purposes are legally permanent. 'A firm that can walk back a commitment has not made one' — the trust converts a promise into a structure rivals (PE roll-ups) won't sign. `[More Co; Diff §7]`
- **Social complexity and causal ambiguity**: An operating culture of 'genuine care' lived in thousands of small moments is socially complex and hard to specify — hard to copy, but also hard to build and verify. `[Diff §7; VRIO §4; More Co]`
- **Where the gain lands**: Engaged, experienced technicians → fewer comebacks, more honest tickets, reputation that fills bays without advertising. Some of that lands in price/volume (WTP); some lands in cost (lower turnover, rework, marketing) — and 'a gain that shows up entirely in cost is an operating improvement, not differentiation.' `[More Co; Diff §3, §8]`
- **Three networks**: Movement (idea; weakest economic case; low cost to run), community (shared place; moderate), vertical (operational overlap; strongest, direct and measurable; high cost). Failure modes: enthusiasm without practice; stays a chamber of commerce; members join for savings and skip the idea. `[More Ex.4]`

### Key terms

- **Perpetual purpose employee ownership trust**: A trust that holds the operating businesses permanently for the benefit of employees; can never be sold; ten percent of profits to charity; liquidation proceeds go to charity. `[More Co]`
- **ESOP**: Employee stock ownership plan: a retirement trust holding company stock; employees accumulate shares and the company must repurchase them at appraised value when they leave (6,411 US ESOP companies, 15.1M participants, 2023). Clegg's objection: it points everyone at valuation, not value creation, and repurchase obligations and outside offers can force a sale. `[More Co; Ex.5]`
- **The MORE idea**: Begin with the person's life, then career, then the company; 'the structure is downstream; it protects the idea.' `[More Co; Ex.1]`
- **Very next step**: Clegg's operating shift: help each person turn a large goal into a step measured in days or weeks; replaced the formal monthly one-on-one with frequent, informal contact plus a monthly manager mastermind. `[More Co]`
- **Five purposes**: Keep It Evergreen; Own This Together; Dare To Care; Pay It Forward (≥10% of distributable profits); Expand The Circle. `[More Ex.2]`
- **The governing number**: Private equity roll-up: valuation. Trust model: value creation. `[More Ex.3]`

### Claims and propositions

- Clegg's theory of value is a claim about causation, not morale: investing in what the person is trying to build → an employee who cares → products and service customers value. `[More Co]`
- When an investor owns the butcher shop, the butcher holds no residual claim on the value created; gains flow to financial owners while risks (layoffs) stay with workers. `[More Co]`
- A coaching program depends on managers, and managers leave; the goal is an operating culture where self-management and genuine care are simply how the place runs. `[More Co]`
- Exhibit 3 tradeoffs: the trust model's source of operating advantage is engagement (tenure, trust, service quality, reputation) vs. PE's scale, consolidation, pricing power and cost reduction; its failure modes are slow growth, founder dependence and capital starvation (it cannot sell equity). `[More Ex.3]`
- Differentiation only counts if the buyer perceives it and pays (or is willing to pay) for it. `[Diff §1]`
- In credence markets differentiation cannot be demonstrated, only signaled, and the signals carry real economic weight; reputation pays — a one-star rating increase raised revenue 5-9%, entirely among independents (zero for chains). `[Diff §2, Ex.3]`

### How it fails / misuse

- **Perceived but not paid**: Employee care that customers never experience as a reason to pay more or come back is a cost, not differentiation. `[Diff §1]`
- **Gain lands only in cost**: If engagement shows up only as lower turnover/rework, it is an operating improvement (still valuable, but a different advantage). `[Diff §8]`
- **Uneven practice**: About a quarter of the workforce consistently in the conversations; the practice is 'real, uneven and unfinished'. `[More Co]`
- **Three internal objections**: Managers lack the skill; employees believe the company has no business in their personal life; production demands crowd out the work. `[More Co]`
- **Capital starvation / founder dependence**: The trust cannot raise equity; growth from cash flow and debt only; Clegg 'has never been busier'. `[More Ex.3; More Co]`

### Case facts that matter

- 2022: four operating businesses moved into a perpetual purpose employee ownership trust; owners (Kevin included) were paid in a negotiated sale to the trust — 'the owner still gets an exit'. *Why it matters:* The structure is a credible commitment that employees and mission will never be subordinated to a future owner. `[More Co; Ex.6]`
- First full fiscal year (2023): employee profit distributions rose from ~$40,000 to ~$400,000, without raising prices. *Why it matters:* Ambiguous evidence: profit grew without price moving — per the brief's diagnostic, that gain landed in cost/volume, not a price premium. `[More Co; Ex.6]`
- Summer 2026: best revenue month in company history; about a quarter of the workforce consistently making progress on what matters most. *Why it matters:* Early, correlational evidence; the case cautions the 25% figure is easy to misread. `[More Co; Ex.6]`
- Gallup H1 2026: 31% of US employees engaged, 51% not engaged, 18% actively disengaged (global: 20/64/16). *Why it matters:* The 'zombie worker' problem the idea targets — and the size of the gap a genuinely engaged workforce could exploit. `[More Co; Ex.5]`
- Holub (regenerative farming) and Felser (Class B camper vans) changed their companies after a conversation about the idea, not the structure. *Why it matters:* Suggests the idea travels across industries (movement network) — but conversion takes many conversations. `[More Co; Ex.4]`
- Clegg's three goals: deepen the practice in the shops (prove it works with blue-collar employees at full coverage), launch the movement, build the networks (movement, community, vertical). *Why it matters:* The decision on the table: sequence and focus of scarce founder attention. `[More Co; Ex.4]`
- 51% of owners of employer businesses are 55 or older; small businesses that outlive founders are increasingly sold to PE roll-ups (buy low, cut costs, consolidate, raise prices, exit). *Why it matters:* The supply of founders facing the exit decision — the market for the model. `[More Co; Ex.5]`

### What a strong student brings to class

- Define terms: product differentiation = the offering itself is better on a dimension buyers value; beyond-product = WTP from the people, trust, ownership and community around the offering (Porter's 'people' engine; the brief's 'trust' and 'identity' sources).
- Better (durability): beyond-product differentiation tends to rest on social complexity, causal ambiguity, path dependence and credible commitment — the four mechanisms that make a premium last — whereas product features are routinely copied (Casper) or normalized (Peloton). A perpetual trust is a commitment a PE-owned rival will not sign.
- Better (fit to the market): in a credence business like auto repair, the product can't be verified, so trust IS the product; honest tickets and fewer comebacks are exactly what the buyer pays for.
- Worse (measurability and verification): the payoff lives in customers' heads and is slow; the case's evidence is mostly cost/volume ($40k → $400k distributions without price increases; best revenue month), not a demonstrated price premium. It's hard to scale (one conversation at a time; 25% coverage), founder-dependent, and capital-constrained.
- Judgment: beyond-product differentiation is more durable but harder to build and prove; it is 'better' where the product is a credence good and the encounter is the product, and only if the firm can show the premium or retention in the accounts. Clegg's sequencing logic: deepen the practice first — a claim that it works anywhere is worth little until it works everywhere in one place.

### Strongest counterargument

- **Argument:** Beyond-product differentiation is just good management (operational effectiveness): engaged employees lower turnover and rework — that's a cost improvement any well-run shop can copy, not a strategy.
- **Response:** Partly right — the brief says a gain that lands only in cost is an operating improvement. But the case's mechanism is trust in a credence market (honest tickets, reputation that fills bays), which lands in WTP and volume. What rivals can't copy is not 'be nice to employees' but the bundle: a structure that can't be sold, a culture lived across thousands of small moments, and years of accumulated reputation (path dependence + social complexity + credible commitment). The burden is on Clegg to show it in retention, comebacks and price realization.

- Profit distributions ~10x ($40k → $400k) without price increases. `[More Co]`

## S11 · Dual Advantage: Cost and Differentiation Together

**Key Question:** How can a firm have both a low-cost and a differentiation advantage? What share of Costco's advantage comes from cost and what share from differentiation? `[Canvas S11]`

**Reading:** No new brief: Porter §7 (hybrid objection and productivity frontier) + Differentiation Brief + cost briefs · **Case:** Costco vs Sam's Club: The Fight Clubs or the Clubs Fight

**Slides:** Session 11 slides are not in the export (Canvas lists 'Slides: Session 11' with no file).

### Framework: Dual advantage, tested against the hybrid objection

Porter says advantage comes in two forms and a position requires trade-offs. The hybrid objection points to firms that seem to win on both. Porter's resolution: (1) many 'hybrids' are operational effectiveness misread as position; (2) genuine dual advantage occurs when a business-model innovation shifts the productivity frontier — real while it lasts; (3) most apparent hybrids are ONE strategy whose trade-offs happen to please customers. The practical test is 'what does this firm refuse to do?'

- **Value stick with both ends moving**: Dual advantage = raising WTP above rivals AND lowering cost below them at the same time; the wedge widens from both ends. `[Diff §1; Porter §2]`
- **Operational effectiveness vs. frontier shift**: Being better on both dimensions may just mean being nearer a frontier every rival can reach; a model that shifts the frontier (Toyota) creates real dual advantage that eventually diffuses. `[Porter §7, Fig.4]`
- **One strategy with fortunate side effects**: Southwest's cost choices also pleased customers; the proof is what it refused. Apply the same test to Costco: limited SKUs (<4,000), pallets, few promotions, willingness to stop selling Coca-Cola, single Kirkland brand. `[Porter §7; Costco]`
- **Where profit lands (decomposition)**: Split operating income into merchandise operations (gross margin − SG&A) and membership fees. Then ask which part of the gap to Sam's comes from lower cost and which from higher WTP (higher fee members pay and renew, more spend per member and per warehouse). `[Costco Ex.1, Ex.3 (site construction)]`
- **Premium tests applied to membership**: Price (Costco's fee is higher: $65/$130 vs. $60/$120), volume at parity (revenue per unit roughly double), retention (renewal 92.3% US & Canada), elasticity (fee raised Sept 2024; fee income +14% in a quarter with the increase a little under half of that). `[Diff §3; Costco]`

### Key terms

- **Dual advantage**: Simultaneously holding a cost advantage and a WTP advantage over rivals. `[Canvas S11; Porter §7]`
- **Hybrid objection**: The criticism that some firms run low cost and high differentiation at once (Toyota, IKEA, Zara, Amazon, Southwest, Trader Joe's). `[Porter §7]`
- **Membership fee income**: Costco $5,323M in FY2025 (Sam's discloses only 'membership and other income', $2,525M FY2026). `[Costco Ex.1, Ex.3, Ex.6]`
- **Low-margin retailer**: Sol Price's self-description: start with product cost and mark up as little as possible so gross margin covers selling costs and leaves a small profit. `[Costco]`
- **Markup rule (reported)**: No branded item marked up more than 14%, no Kirkland item more than 15% — reported in 2005, never in a filing. `[Costco]`

### Claims and propositions

- Apparent hybrids usually turn out to be one strategy whose trade-offs please customers; genuine dual advantage needs a business-model innovation that shifts the frontier, and it is real while it lasts. `[Porter §7]`
- A firm that can name three things rivals do that it deliberately won't do has a position. `[Porter §7]`
- Costco's merchandise operations earn gross margin (11.12%) above SG&A (9.25%) — a positive 1.87% of net sales — and membership fees ($5,323M) are about 51% of operating income ($10,383M). `[Costco Ex.1 (computed)]`
- Sam's Club: operating income ($2,442M) minus membership and other income ($2,525M) = −$83M, so its gross margin roughly equals (slightly below) its operating expenses — essentially all of Sam's profit comes from fees and other income. `[Costco Ex.3 (computed; case Assignment Q2)]`
- Sinegal on wages: 'This is not altruistic. This is good business.' On horizon: 'We want to build a company that will still be here 50 and 60 years from now.' `[Costco]`
- Kirkland's function per Costco's CMO: 'a tremendous negotiating tool.' `[Costco]`

### How it fails / misuse

- **Calling operational effectiveness a hybrid**: If Costco's edge were only better practice, Sam's (with Walmart's buying power) could reach the same frontier. `[Porter §7]`
- **Buying power that doesn't show up**: Sam's buys alongside a $706B parent yet earns nothing on merchandise — scale in bargaining is the weakest scale mechanism (Scale brief) and Sam's vendor relationships are described as poor (K7 Design judgment). `[Costco; Scale 'Bargaining']`
- **Non-comparable data**: Fiscal years differ; Costco reports total employees, Sam's frontline only; Sam's 'membership and other income' includes other income; Costco square footage is approximated. `[Costco Ex.4 notes]`
- **Strategy that tracks the rival**: Sam's leadership turned over five times since 2005; its plan (double members, remodel all clubs, 15 new clubs/yr) produced 2 net clubs in 16 months while Costco opened ~10x that. `[Costco]`

### Case facts that matter

- Costco FY2025: net sales $269,912M, membership fees $5,323M, total revenue $275,235M, merchandise costs $239,886M, SG&A $24,966M, operating income $10,383M, net income $8,099M; gross margin 11.12% and SG&A 9.25% of net sales. *Why it matters:* The inputs for the decomposition and the per-warehouse income statement (Assignment Q1-Q2). `[Costco Ex.1]`
- Sam's FY2026 (Jan 2026): net sales $93,015M, membership and other income $2,525M, operating income $2,442M, 601 clubs, 80,502k sq ft. Walmart does not disclose Sam's gross profit or operating expenses. *Why it matters:* What you can infer: GM% − Opex% ≈ −0.09% of net sales. `[Costco Ex.3]`
- Per unit: Costco US $318.0M revenue per warehouse vs. Sam's $159.0M; revenue per sq ft ~$2,164 vs. $1,187; employees per unit 355 vs. 166; revenue per employee $897k vs. $955k (definitions differ). *Why it matters:* Roughly double the throughput per box — a 'volume at parity' signal of higher WTP and a driver of lower unit cost (fixed box cost spread over twice the sales). `[Costco Ex.4]`
- Wages: Costco ~$32.00/hr average, starting ≥$20.00, 94% retention after one year; Sam's average frontline >$19 (Nov 2024), turnover undisclosed. *Why it matters:* Costco pays more yet runs lower SG&A% — wages buy productivity and retention (a cost advantage built on the people engine). `[Costco]`
- Membership: Costco $65 / $130 Executive (raised Sept 2024); 81.0M paid members (82.9M by May 2026); 38.7M Executive members = ~73.6% of worldwide net sales; renewal 92.3% US & Canada, 89.8% worldwide. Sam's $60 / $120 (raised May 2026); discloses none of these. *Why it matters:* WTP evidence: members pay more for the right to shop and renew at very high rates. `[Costco Ex.6]`
- Assortment: Costco <4,000 SKUs in warehouse (9,000-10,000 online); Sam's ~4,000 in club (~20,000 online). Kirkland ~600 products, ~28% of sales (2022); Member's Mark ~30% (press estimate), consolidated from ~20 brands in 2017 — 22 years after Kirkland. *Why it matters:* Similar formats — the difference is execution, discipline and time. `[Costco; Ex.4]`
- Signature items: $1.50 hot dog since the mid-1980s (built hot dog plants rather than raise the price; ~200M sold in FY2023); $4.99 rotisserie chicken (willing to give up $30-40M of margin; built a poultry operation); stopped selling Coca-Cola in 2009 over pricing; ended Amex exclusivity in 2015. *Why it matters:* Credible commitments to low prices and willingness to refuse — passes Porter's trade-off test. `[Costco]`
- Comparable sales: Sam's ex-fuel slowed from 7.0% to ~3.8-4.4%; Costco US adjusted held 5.9-8.6%. Sam's Q2 FY27 operating income $678M was helped by tariff refunds; prior-year figure restated without explanation. *Why it matters:* Momentum and disclosure quality both favor Costco. `[Costco Ex.5]`
- Leadership: Costco CEOs Sinegal (28 yrs), Jelinek (12), Vachris (40+ yrs at Costco, started as a forklift operator); CFO Galanti ~40 yrs. Sam's: McMillon, Furner, McLay, Nicholas since 2005. *Why it matters:* Consistency test: Costco's incentives, hiring and capital allocation have pointed the same way for decades. `[Costco]`

### What a strong student brings to class

- How a firm can have both: Costco's cost advantage (low SG&A%, few SKUs, pallets, huge volume per SKU and per box) funds low markups; low prices + Kirkland quality + high-wage, low-turnover staff raise members' WTP (they pay a higher fee, renew at 92%, and spend more per visit); higher volume per box lowers cost further. The two sides reinforce each other — a business model that shifted the frontier (Porter §7, part 2) built from choices made for cost whose trade-offs customers love (part 3).
- Decompose Costco's operating income: ~49% from merchandise operations (GM 11.12% − SG&A 9.25% = 1.87% of sales ≈ $5,060M) and ~51% from membership fees ($5,323M). Fees are the purest measure of WTP for the 'right to shop'; the merchandise spread is where cost efficiency shows up (prices low enough that the margin barely covers SG&A).
- Versus Sam's: Sam's merchandise spread is ≈ 0 (−$83M), so its whole profit is fees and other income. Costco's lead over Sam's sits mostly in merchandise operations (cost per dollar of sales and throughput per box) plus a fee/renewal premium. A defensible answer to 'what share?': state assumptions — e.g., treat the fee premium and the throughput premium (sales per warehouse at parity) as differentiation, and the SG&A-rate gap as cost — then compute (the site's decomposition tool makes each assumption adjustable).
- Answer Q5 (higher fee, higher wages, lower shelf prices all true): fees carry about half the profit, letting markups stay tiny; high wages buy productivity and retention so SG&A per dollar stays low; and throughput per box is roughly double, spreading fixed cost.
- Answer Q4 (Walmart's buying power doesn't help Sam's much): bargaining is the weakest scale mechanism, Costco's volume per SKU (concentrated on <4,000 items) rivals Sam's even at lower total company size, Kirkland is a negotiating tool, and Sam's lacks the consistency (leadership turnover, poor vendor relations) to exploit the parent's scale — an Organization problem in VRIO terms.

### Strongest counterargument

- **Argument:** Costco has no differentiation advantage — it's a pure cost leader. The 'WTP' is just low prices; members pay the fee because the savings exceed it, not because they value Costco more.
- **Response:** Porter would partly agree (one strategy, fortunate side effects). But the evidence of WTP above Sam's at near-equal prices is in the accounts: a higher fee, 92% renewal, Executive members (~74% of sales) paying $130, and roughly double sales per warehouse — the brief's 'volume at parity' and 'retention' tests. Kirkland quality and trust (credible commitments like the $1.50 hot dog) raise WTP independent of price. The honest answer: the advantage starts as cost and converts into WTP; the split depends on assumptions you must state.
