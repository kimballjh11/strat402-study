## S03 — Industry Structure (Markets to Pursue)

**Learning objectives**

- `S03-LO1` Know and understand the following concepts: C4 ratio, HHI *[course-data.js Session 3 'Read: Chapter 7'; S03 slides p.3]*
- `S03-LO2` Know how to calculate various measures of industry concentration and how they relate to market competitiveness *[course-data.js Session 3]*
- `S03-LO3` Understand the limitations of concentration measures *[course-data.js Session 3]*

**Key terms**

- **Concentrated industry** — An industry is 'concentrated' when a few large firms control the industry (e.g., Auto, Airline, etc.). *[S03 slides p.4]*
- **Concentration vs. competitiveness** — Industry concentration and market competitiveness are inversely related (i.e., monopoly at 100% vs. perfect competition at ≈ 0%). *[S03 slides p.4]*
- **Four-firm concentration ratio (C4)** — The fraction of total sales generated by the four largest firms in the industry (ranges from 0 = extremely competitive to 1 = highly concentrated). *[S03 slides p.5]*
- **Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)** — The sum of the squared market shares of firms in a given industry multiplied by 10,000 (ranges from 0 = extremely competitive to 10,000 = monopoly). *[S03 slides p.6]*

**Formulas**

- C4: `C_4 = \frac{S_1+S_2+S_3+S_4}{S_T} = w_1+w_2+w_3+w_4` *[S03 slides p.5]*
- HHI: `HHI = 10{,}000\sum w_i^2 = 10{,}000\sum\left(\frac{S_i}{S_T}\right)^2` *[S03 slides p.6]*

**Worked examples**

- **US mobile telecom 2013** — Verizon 42%, AT&T 33%, Sprint 16%, T-Mobile 3%; remaining 6% treated as one firm → C4 = 94%; HHI = (0.1764+0.1089+0.0256+0.0009+0.0036)×10,000 = 3,154 *[S03 slides p.7]*
- **US mobile telecom 2014** — 37/31/14/12 + 6 other as one firm → C4 = 94%; HHI = 2,706 *[S03 slides p.9]*
- **Allegiant airports (Dec 2025 BTS data)** — OAK vs SFO, HOU vs IAH, MDW vs ORD shares → OAK HHI 7,323 C4 95.4% vs SFO 2,765 / 76.8%; HOU 8,939 / 98.8% vs IAH 3,602 / 78.7%; MDW 8,632 / 99.2% vs ORD 2,503 / 78.2%. Allegiant cities are more concentrated, consistent with its strategy of avoiding competition at smaller airports. *[S03 workbook, Solution!D42:H65, B69]*

**cases**

- US domestic airlines 2025: C4 = 64.4%, HHI = 1,162 (S03 slides p.12)
- Route-level HHIs 2025: National 1,162; LAS-LAX 1,620; ATL-MCO 5,324; LAX-SFO 2,104; ORD-LGA 3,227; LAX-JFK 3,433 (S03 slides p.14)
- US autos 2025 all firms: GM 18, Toyota 16, Ford 14, Hyundai/Kia 11; C4 59%, HHI 2,578. Domestic only: GM 50, Ford 39, Tesla 10, Rivian 1; C4 100%, HHI 4,122 (S03 slides p.15)
- Change in avg top-4 share by sector 1997-2017: increasing lack of competition (S03 slides p.10)
- Allegiant business model: low-cost, leisure-only, ~75% of routes no nonstop competition, ancillary $76.35/passenger in 2025 (S03 workbook Solution!B18)

**traps**

- Use shares as decimals before squaring (42% → 0.42), then ×10,000
- Treat 'remaining share' as one firm only when told (professor's telecom convention)
- C4 can be identical while HHI differs (telecom 2013 vs 2014: both 94%, HHI 3,154 vs 2,706)
- National HHI understates route/local concentration

## S04 — Industry Analysis (Markets to Pursue)

**Learning objectives**

- `S04-LO1` Know and understand the following concepts: rivalry, substitute, barriers to entry, buyer power, supplier power, attractiveness of an industry, switching costs, backward integration, forward integration, network effects *[course-data.js Session 4 'Read: Chapter 2'; S04 slides p.11]*
- `S04-LO2` Know and understand how to correctly identify a firm's industries and markets *[course-data.js Session 4]*
- `S04-LO3` Be able to identify and evaluate the five major forces that shape average industry profitability to evaluate the overall attractiveness of an industry *[course-data.js Session 4]*
- `S04-LO4` Be able to identify the factors in the general environment that affect firm and industry profitability *[course-data.js Session 4]*
- `S04-LO5` Know and understand where to find information necessary to perform industry analysis *[course-data.js Session 4]*

**Key terms**

- **Rivalry** — Competition between firms within the incumbent industry. Lower levels of rivalry tend to have a positive effect on industry profits. *[Five Forces Tool, Glossary!B1]*
- **Buyer power** — The relative power of companies and/or consumers who purchase products or services from the incumbent industry. When buyers have a lot of power they negotiate for lower prices and/or other concessions. High buyer power makes the focus industry less attractive. *[Five Forces Tool, Glossary!B12]*
- **Supplier power** — The relative power of firms who sell materials/products to the incumbent industry. High supplier power has a negative effect on the profitability of the incumbent industry. *[Five Forces Tool, Glossary!B25]*
- **Threat of new entrants / barriers to entry** — The possibility of newcomers entering and capturing market share from incumbents. If barriers to entry are high, the threat of new entrants is low, making the industry more attractive for incumbents. *[Five Forces Tool, Glossary!B32]*
- **Substitute** — A product that customers could switch to that is not necessarily in direct rivalry with incumbents, e.g., driving instead of flying (Honda is not in direct rivalry with Delta). Fulfills the same job in a different way. *[Five Forces Tool, Glossary!B47; S04 slides p.23]*
- **Switching costs** — How hard it is for customers to switch from one incumbent's product to another. The harder it is, the more attractive the industry. *[Five Forces Tool, Glossary!B7]*
- **Backward integration** — A buyer entering the incumbent industry and becoming its own supplier. The easier it is for buyers to backward integrate, the more power buyers have. *[Five Forces Tool, Glossary!B17]*
- **Forward integration** — A supplier entering the incumbent industry. If it is easy for suppliers to forward integrate, suppliers have greater power relative to incumbents. *[Five Forces Tool, Glossary!B31]*
- **Network effects** — A product becomes more valuable to consumers as more consumers use it; strong network effects lower the threat of new entrants. *[Five Forces Tool, Glossary!B42]*
- **Attractiveness of an industry** — Evaluated from the incumbent's point of view; the five forces shape average industry profitability. Scored 1 (least) to 5 (most attractive) in the tool. *[Five Forces Tool, Instructions!A2; S04 Solution]*

**Worked examples**

- **S04 Solution: Storage & Warehouse Leasing (self-storage) Five Forces** —  → Rivalry 2.36 (moderately unattractive); Buyer power 3.33 (moderately attractive); Supplier power 4.78 (very attractive); Threat of entry 2.83 (moderately unattractive); Substitutes 3.40 (moderately attractive); Overall 3.34 *[S04 Solution, Five Forces!G22,G45,G59,G86,G101,G116]*
- **Scoring mechanics** —  → Each component rated 1-5 (whole numbers). Force score = weighted average of component scores (weights col I); component with sub-components = weighted avg of sub-ratings (sub-weights col K). Overall = weighted avg of the five force scores. Bands: 1-2 very unattractive, 2-3 moderately unattractive, 3-4 moderately attractive, 4-5 very attractive. *[S04 Solution, Calculations Hub!K21..K107; Five Forces!A23]*

**cases**

- Theory Meets Practice (WSJ Aug 30, 2025): Thompson Traders copper bathtubs & tariffs; Lowe's/Home Depot set retail price, agreed only to 4-5% increase — which party has leverage? Who eats the cost of tariffs? (buyer power) (S04 slides pp.8-10)
- Meta 10-K Item 1A risk factors → PEST; reputation loss could increase rivalry, buyer power (advertisers), and substitutes; Aug 2026 update: $18B settlement with 48 state AGs, under-18 limits; last $5.3B contingent on TikTok/YouTube agreeing (Skills: Analyzing the General Environment page)
- Most profitable industries 2015-2024 by ROIC: Internet Software & Services 33%; Interactive Media 27%; Software 22%; Specialty Retail 19% (Home Depot, Lowe's, Best Buy); Household Products 17%. Least: Tech Hardware -9%; Insurance 1%; Banks 2%; Energy Equipment 2%; Office REITs 3% (S04 slides pp.27-28)
- Neighbor, 'the Airbnb of storage' (videos) — a substitute/new entrant for self-storage (S04 module)
- IBISWorld industry reports as a data source (S04 slides p.24; IBISWorld 44411 report)

**traps**

- Ratings run 1 = least attractive to 5 = most attractive for incumbents — high buyer power → LOW rating
- Segments ≠ buyers when rating buyer concentration
- Use forward-looking growth rates
- Substitutes do the same job a different way; rivals do the same job the same way

## S05 — Math & Excel Review (Unique Value to Offer)

**Learning objectives**

- `S05-LO1` Know and understand the following mathematical concepts: function, slope, derivative, power rule, partial derivative *[course-data.js Session 5 'Read: STRAT 402 Math Review']*
- `S05-LO2` Know and understand how to use calculus to optimize managerial decisions *[course-data.js Session 5]*
- `S05-LO3` Know and understand how to do the following in Excel: create a scatterplot; adjust the horizontal and vertical axes to fit the range of the data; add a title and label the x- and y-axes; add a trendline to a plot; use Solver to optimize managerial decisions *[course-data.js Session 5 'Watch: Essential Excel Skills']*

**Key terms**

- **Function** — A quantitative formula that defines the relationship between a set of inputs and outputs. *[Math Review p.1]*
- **Slope** — The rise over the run, or the change in y over the change in x (Δy/Δx). For y = mx + b, m is the slope and b is the intercept. *[Math Review p.3]*
- **Derivative** — A function that evaluates the instantaneous rate of change of some original function, i.e., it tells you the slope (Δy/Δx) at any given value of x. For a straight line, the slope and derivative are equal; for a curve, it is the slope of the tangent line at that point. *[Math Review p.5; S05 slides pp.19-20]*
- **Power rule** — For y = x^a, dy/dx = a·x^(a−1). Bring the exponent down in front and subtract 1 from the exponent; constants (no x) drop out. *[Math Review p.5; S05 slides p.27]*
- **Partial derivative** — The derivative of a function with respect to one individual independent variable while the other variables are held constant. Written ∂y/∂x. Used to isolate the effect of specific variables. *[Math Review p.10; S05 slides p.43]*

**Formulas**

- Line: `y = mx + b` *[S05 slides p.15]*
- Power rule: `y = x^a \Rightarrow \frac{dy}{dx} = a x^{a-1}` *[S05 slides p.27]*
- Three-step optimization: `\text{1) } f'(x) \quad \text{2) } f'(x)=0 \quad \text{3) solve for } x` *[Math Review p.7]*

**Worked examples**

- **Revenue-maximizing price** — R(P) = 100P − 2P² → P* = 25 (revenue = 1,250) *[Math Review pp.7-8; S05 slides p.36]*
- **Cost-minimizing quantity** — ATC(Q) = 2Q² − 100Q + 1,400 → Q* = 25 *[Math Review pp.8-9; S05 slides p.40]*
- **Derivative table** — y = x² + 3 → dy/dx = 2x: x=0 → 0; x=1 → 2; x=2 → 4; x=3 → 6 *[Math Review p.6]*
- **Practice derivative** — y = −5x³ + 4x + 1 → dy/dx = −15x² + 4 *[S05 slides p.32]*
- **iPhone partial derivatives** — Q_IP = 1,000 − 4P_IP + 2P_SG + 100A_IP + 500I² → ∂Q/∂P_IP = −4 (each $1 price increase → 4 fewer units); ∂Q/∂P_SG = 2 (a $10 Galaxy increase → +20 units); ∂Q/∂A = 100; ∂Q/∂I = 1,000I *[Math Review p.10; S05 slides p.45]*
- **Sundance season passes** — Q = 6,000 − 12P → P* = $250 *[S05 Optional Practice p.4]*

**traps**

- Constants vanish when differentiating (y = x² + 3 → 2x)
- Setting the derivative to zero finds max OR min; check the shape (−P² opens down = max; +Q² opens up = min)
- Partial derivative: treat other variables as constants
- Slide p.30 table shows y=11 at x=3; the Math Review and p.29 correctly show 12

## S06 — Cost & Economies of Scale (Unique Value to Offer)

**Learning objectives**

- `S06-LO1` Know and understand the sources of cost advantage *[course-data.js Session 6 'Read: Chapter 5']*
- `S06-LO2` Know and understand the following economic concepts and how they relate to one another: fixed costs, variable costs, total cost, average fixed cost, average variable cost, average total cost, marginal cost, economies of scale *[course-data.js Session 6]*
- `S06-LO3` Understand the strategic implications of economies of scale (i.e., when does it make sense to grow?) *[course-data.js Session 6]*

**Key terms**

- **Cost function** — Defines the relationship between production (Q) and total cost (C). Total cost can be broken into fixed (FC) and variable costs (VC). *[S06 Video Slides p.12]*
- **Fixed cost (FC)** — The part of the cost function that does not change with Q (the constant term). *[S06 Video Slides p.14; CQ3 Q1 feedback]*
- **Variable cost (VC)** — Every term that changes with Q. *[S06 Video Slides p.15; CQ3 Q2 feedback]*
- **Average fixed cost (AFC)** — AFC = FC/Q *[S06 Video Slides p.17]*
- **Average variable cost (AVC)** — AVC = VC(Q)/Q *[S06 Video Slides p.18]*
- **Average total cost (ATC)** — ATC = AVC + AFC (= C/Q) *[S06 Video Slides p.19]*
- **Marginal cost (MC)** — The cost of producing an additional unit of output, or the change in cost attributable to the last unit of output. MC = ΔC/ΔQ = dC/dQ. *[S06 Video Slides p.20]*
- **Short run vs. long run** — In the short run, capital (e.g., number of grills) is fixed and labor is variable; in the long run a manager is free to adjust all inputs — all costs are variable. *[S06 Video Slides p.27]*
- **Long-run average cost curve (LRAC)** — Defines the minimum average cost of producing alternative levels of output, allowing for optimal selection of fixed and variable factors of production. *[S06 Video Slides p.29]*
- **Economies of scale** — Exist when long-run average costs decline as output is increased. *[S06 Video Slides p.29]*
- **Diseconomies of scale** — Exist when long-run average costs rise as output is increased. *[S06 Video Slides p.29]*
- **Learning and experience** — 'Practice makes perfect': firms become more efficient through practice; costs fall with cumulative output over time, whereas economies of scale come from the volume produced in a given period. *[CQ3 Q9 feedback]*
- **Scale curve** — Cost per unit (y) plotted against scale (x), fit with a power function y = a·x^b. Slope of the curve = 2^b = cost multiplier per doubling; % drop per doubling = 1 − 2^b. *[S06 workbook Solution!B49-E84; Cost Advantage Part 1 step 5]*

**Formulas**

- Total cost: `C(Q) = FC + VC(Q)` *[S06 Video p.12,16]*
- Averages: `AFC=\frac{FC}{Q},\; AVC=\frac{VC(Q)}{Q},\; ATC = AFC + AVC` *[S06 Video pp.17-19]*
- Marginal cost: `MC = \frac{\Delta C}{\Delta Q} = \frac{dC}{dQ}` *[S06 Video p.20]*
- Power-law scale curve: `ATC = a\,Q^{b},\quad \text{cost after doubling} = 2^{b}\times\text{cost}` *[S06 workbook Solution!D83:E84]*
- DuPont: `ROA = ROS\times AT,\; ROE = ROA\times\text{Leverage}` *[S06 slides p.6 (relationships implied by the table)]*

**Worked examples**

- **J. Dawgs one grill** — C(Q) = 0.00002Q³ − 0.005Q² + 0.8Q + 8; Q = 100 → FC = 8; VC = 20 − 50 + 80 = $50; C = $58; AFC = $0.08; AVC = $0.50; ATC = $0.58; MC = 0.6 − 1 + 0.8 = $0.40 *[S06 Video Slides pp.14-21]*
- **J. Dawgs production table** — K = 1 grill at $8, wage $10; Q at L = 0..9: 0,10,28,54,84,116,146,172,190,200 → FC = 8; VC = 10L; C = 8, 18, 28, ..., 98. Increasing then diminishing marginal returns. *[S06 Video Slides pp.4-10]*
- **J. Dawgs two grills (CQ3)** — C = 0.000002Q³ − 0.0005Q² + 0.25Q + 16; Q = 400 → AFC $0.04; VC $148; AVC $0.37; MC $0.81; MC minimized at Q ≈ 83 *[S06 Video p.24; Comprehension Quiz 3 Q1-5]*
- **Whole Foods / Kroger / Safeway FY2014** — Revenue 14,194 / 108,465 / 36,360; COGS 9,150 / 85,512 / 26,648; SG&A 4,110 / 19,816 / 9,148; Op income 934 / 3,137 / 534; NI 579 / 1,728 / 113; Assets 5,744 / 30,556 / 13,377; Liab 1,931 / 25,114 / 7,927; Equity 3,813 / 5,442 / 5,450 ($M) → ROS 4.1% / 1.6% / 0.3%; AT 2.5 / 3.5 / 2.7; Leverage 1.5 / 5.6 / 2.5; ROA 10.1% / 5.7% / 0.8%; ROE 15.2% / 31.8% / 2.1% *[S06 slides p.6]*

## S07 — Demand & Consumer Surplus (Unique Value to Offer)

**Learning objectives**

- `S07-LO1` Know and understand the following concepts: market demand curve, law of demand, change in quantity demanded, shift in demand, normal good, inferior good, substitute, complement, demand function, inverse demand function, consumer surplus *[course-data.js Session 7 'Read: Chapter 2: Demand']*
- `S07-LO2` Know and understand how to construct and plot a demand curve *[course-data.js Session 7]*
- `S07-LO3` Know how to calculate the revenue maximizing price using calculus *[course-data.js Session 7]*
- `S07-LO4` Know how to calculate consumer surplus and describe what it means *[course-data.js Session 7]*
- `S07-LO5` Know and understand the four major categories or sources of product or service differentiation *[course-data.js Session 7 (taught in the 'Differentiation Advantage' LearnStrategy video — transcript not in materials)]*

**Key terms**

- **Market demand curve** — Shows the total quantity of a good that all consumers in the market would purchase at each price, holding other factors constant. *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 2 topic)]*
- **Law of demand** — Price and quantity demanded are inversely related: a price increase leads to a decrease in quantity demanded (and vice versa), all else equal. *[S08 slides p.16 (applied); GENERAL ECON wording]*
- **Change in quantity demanded** — A movement ALONG a demand curve caused by a change in the good's own price. *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 2 topic)]*
- **Shift in demand** — The whole curve moves (left = decrease, right = increase) because a non-price determinant changed: income, prices of related goods, advertising/consumer tastes, population, expectations. *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 2 topic); determinants listed in S05 slides p.41 and Math Review p.9]*
- **Normal good** — Demand increases when income rises; the coefficient on income in a demand function is positive. *[CQ4 Q2 feedback (sign logic); Regression Exercise step 6 (Kimball)]*
- **Inferior good** — Demand decreases when income rises; the coefficient on income is negative. *[CQ4 Q2 feedback]*
- **Substitute** — An increase in the price of good y raises demand for good x; the coefficient on Py is positive (CPE > 0). *[CQ4 Q1 feedback; S08 slides p.27, p.29]*
- **Complement** — An increase in the price of good y lowers demand for good x; the coefficient on Py is negative (CPE < 0), e.g., milk and cereal. *[S08 slides p.27]*
- **Demand function** — Quantity as a function of price (and other variables), e.g., Qx = 10,000 − 4Px + 5Py − 0.2I. *[CQ4 Q3]*
- **Inverse demand function** — Price on the left, as a function of quantity: solve the demand function for P. E.g., Qx = 4,500 − 4Px → Px = 1,125 − 0.25Qx. *[CQ4 Q3 feedback]*
- **Consumer surplus** — The area under the demand curve and above the price: the value consumers receive beyond what they pay. For linear demand, a triangle = ½ × quantity × (choke price − price). *[CQ4 Q4 feedback; meaning = GENERAL ECON]*
- **Choke price** — The price where quantity demanded hits zero (the vertical intercept of demand). *[CQ4 Q4 feedback]*

**Formulas**

- Inverse demand: `Q = a - bP \;\Rightarrow\; P = \frac{a}{b} - \frac{1}{b}Q` *[CQ4 Q3]*
- Revenue: `R(P) = P\cdot Q(P)` *[S07 slides p.30]*
- Revenue-maximizing price: `\frac{dR}{dP} = 0` *[S07 slides p.30]*
- Consumer surplus (linear): `CS = \tfrac12\,Q(P)\,(P_{choke}-P)` *[CQ4 Q4 feedback]*

**Worked examples**

## S08 — Elasticity & Price Discrimination (Unique Value to Offer)

**Learning objectives**

- `S08-LO1` Know and understand the following concepts: own price elasticity, inelastic and elastic demand, the total revenue test, cross price elasticity *[course-data.js Session 8 'Read: Chapter 3']*
- `S08-LO2` Know how to calculate the various forms of elasticity and understand how they relate to strategy *[course-data.js Session 8]*

**Key terms**

- **Elasticity** — A measure of responsiveness of one variable to changes in another variable; the percentage change in one variable relative to changes in another; a measure of sensitivity (e.g., price-sensitive customers). *[S08 slides p.15]*
- **Own-price elasticity (OPE)** — OPE = %ΔQx/%ΔPx = (∂Qx/∂Px)(Px/Qx). OPE will always be negative (law of demand). *[S08 slides p.16]*
- **Elastic demand** — OPE < −1. Consumers are relatively price sensitive. *[S08 slides p.17]*
- **Inelastic demand** — −1 < OPE < 0. Consumers are relatively price insensitive. *[S08 slides p.17]*
- **Unitary elastic** — OPE = −1. *[S08 slides p.17]*
- **Total revenue test** — (1) If demand is elastic (OPE < −1), a price increase (decrease) leads to a decrease (increase) in total revenue. (2) If inelastic (−1 < OPE < 0), a price increase (decrease) leads to an increase (decrease) in total revenue. (3) Total revenue is maximized when demand is unitary elastic. *[S08 slides p.18]*
- **Cross-price elasticity (CPE)** — CPE = %ΔQx/%ΔPy = (∂Qx/∂Py)(Py/Qx). Can be negative or positive: positive → substitutes (Dr Pepper vs. Mr. Pibb); negative → complements (milk and cereal). *[S08 slides p.27]*
- **Income elasticity** — %ΔQx/%ΔI; positive for normal goods, negative for inferior goods. *[Type listed on S08 slides p.15; formula = GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 3 topic)]*
- **Advertising elasticity** — %ΔQx/%ΔA — responsiveness of quantity to advertising spending. *[Type listed on S08 slides p.15; formula = GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 3 topic)]*

**Formulas**

- OPE (arc / algebraic): `OPE = \frac{\%\Delta Q_x}{\%\Delta P_x} = \frac{(Q_{new}-Q_{old})/Q_{old}}{(P_{new}-P_{old})/P_{old}}` *[S08 slides p.23]*
- OPE (point / calculus): `OPE = \frac{\partial Q_x}{\partial P_x}\cdot\frac{P_x}{Q_x}` *[S08 slides p.16, p.25]*
- CPE: `CPE = \frac{\%\Delta Q_x}{\%\Delta P_y} = \frac{\partial Q_x}{\partial P_y}\cdot\frac{P_y}{Q_x}` *[S08 slides p.27]*
- Predict % change: `\%\Delta Q_x = E \times \%\Delta P` *[S08 slides p.31; CQ4 Q8]*
- Income elasticity: `E_I = \frac{\partial Q_x}{\partial I}\cdot\frac{I}{Q_x}` *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 3)]*
- Log-linear demand: `\ln Q = \beta_0 + \beta_P \ln P + \dots \Rightarrow OPE = \beta_P` *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 3) — not in course slides]*

**Worked examples**

- **Redbox OPE (algebraic)** — Oct 31, 2011: $1.00 → $1.20; 5,000 → 4,500 rentals per kiosk → OPE = (−0.10)/(0.20) = −0.50 → inelastic at $1.00 *[S08 slides pp.21-23]*
- **Redbox OPE (calculus)** — Qx = −3,728Px + 8,845; Px = $1.00 → OPE = −3,728 × 1/5,117 = −0.73 *[S08 slides p.25]*
- **CPE exercise** — Qx = 500 − 5Px + 4Py; Px = 25, Py = 20 → y is a substitute; Qx = 455; CPE = 4 × 20/455 = 0.18; a 10% increase in Py → +1.8% Qx *[S08 slides pp.28-31]*
- **Dr Pepper OPE table** — Q = 30,000 − 20,000P at P = 0, 0.25, ..., 1.50 → OPE: 0, −0.2, −0.5, −1, −2, −5, −∞; revenue 0, 6,250, 10,000, 11,250, 10,000, 6,250, 0; max at P = $0.75, Q = 15,000, R = $11,250, OPE = −1; elastic above $0.75, inelastic below *[S08 workbook, OPE - Solution!B14:E20 and comments B61, B70]*
- **Dr Pepper / Mr. Pibb CPE** — CPE for Dr Pepper = 1.2; CPE for Mr. Pibb = 2.0; 25% price cut → DP cuts price 25% → Mr. Pibb quantity −50%; Mr. Pibb cuts 25% → Dr Pepper −30%; Dr Pepper is a much stronger substitute for Mr. Pibb than vice versa *[S08 workbook, CPE - Solution comments B15, B26, B37]*
- **Netflix 2011 (CQ4 Q10)** — Expected 25M, actual 23.8M; price $10 → $16 → %ΔQ = −4.8%; %ΔP = 60%; OPE = −0.08 → inelastic *[CQ4 Q10 feedback]*

## S09 — Forecasting Demand Using Linear Regression (Unique Value to Offer)

**Learning objectives**

- `S09-LO1` Know and understand the following statistical terms: regression coefficient, standard error, t-statistic, p-value, R-squared *[course-data.js Session 9]*
- `S09-LO2` Know how to run a regression model in Excel *[course-data.js Session 9]*
- `S09-LO3` Know and understand how to interpret and use regression output *[course-data.js Session 9]*

**Key terms**

- **OLS regression** — The line that minimizes the sum of squared deviations between the line and the actual data points. Y = a + bX, where a and b are parameters that define the intercept and slope. Objective: estimate the parameters that define the true relationship between Y and X(s). *[S09 slides p.20]*
- **Regression coefficients** — Estimates of the true parameters that define the relationship between dependent and independent variables. Interpretation: 'A one-unit increase in x leads to (magnitude of coefficient) increase/decrease (sign of coefficient) in y.' *[S09 slides p.24]*
- **Standard error** — The standard deviation of the parameter estimate; a measure of how noisy our estimate is. The smaller/greater the standard error, the more/less precise your parameter estimate. *[S09 slides p.26]*
- **t-statistic** — The ratio of the parameter estimate (regression coefficient) to its standard error. Generally, t-statistics greater than 2 in absolute value indicate the coefficient is statistically significant (not equal to zero). *[S09 slides p.29]*
- **p-value** — An alternative measure of statistical significance; per the slides, it 'tells you the probability that a true parameter that you estimated is equal to zero.' By convention, a coefficient is statistically significant when its p-value is less than 0.05. *[S09 slides p.31]*
- **R-squared** — Coefficient of determination; a measure of model fit equal to explained variation over total variation; ranges from 0 to 1. 'An R-squared of ___ indicates that ___% of the variation in y can be explained by variation in the x's.' *[S09 slides p.33]*
- **Multiple R** — Square root of R-squared. *[CQ5 Q2 feedback]*
- **Adjusted R-squared** — R-squared penalized for the number of variables the model uses. *[CQ5 Q2 feedback]*
- **Isolating effects** — Multiple regression isolates the effect of one variable holding others constant (the airline fare example; Netflix growth and season removed). *[S09 slides pp.9-14]*

**Formulas**

- Simple regression line: `Y = a + bX` *[S09 slides p.20]*
- t-stat: `t = \frac{\text{coefficient}}{\text{standard error}}` *[S09 slides p.29]*
- R-squared: `R^2 = \frac{SS_{Regression}}{SS_{Total}}` *[S09 slides p.33 (explained/total)]*
- Elasticity from linear regression: `OPE = b_P\cdot\frac{\bar P}{\bar Q}` *[S09 workbook Solution!A57]*

**Worked examples**

## S10 — Profit Maximization Across Market Structures (Unique Value to Offer)

**Learning objectives**

- `S10-LO1` Know and understand the following economic terms: marginal analysis, marginal benefit, marginal cost, marginal revenue, monopoly, monopolistic competition, perfectly competitive market *[course-data.js Session 10]*
- `S10-LO2` Be able to identify when a firm operates in an industry that is perfectly competitive, monopolistically competitive, or a monopoly *[course-data.js Session 10]*
- `S10-LO3` Be able to identify the sources of (and strategies for obtaining) monopoly power *[course-data.js Session 10]*
- `S10-LO4` Know how to calculate the profit-maximizing levels of price and quantity for perfectly competitive, monopolistically competitive, and monopoly firms using marginal analysis *[course-data.js Session 10]*

**Key terms**

- **Marginal analysis** — Deciding by comparing the benefit and cost of one more unit: keep going until the marginal benefit (MB) equals the marginal cost (MC). MB = MC. *[S10 slides p.14]*
- **Marginal benefit (MB)** — The additional benefit (e.g., revenue) from one more unit of an activity. *[S10 slides p.15 (table); wording GENERAL ECON]*
- **Marginal cost (MC)** — The additional cost of one more unit (dC/dQ). *[S06 Video p.20; S10 slides p.15]*
- **Marginal revenue (MR)** — The additional revenue collected when one additional unit of production or service is rendered. MR = dR/dQ. *[S10 slides p.17]*
- **Perfectly competitive market** — Many firms; homogeneous goods (commodities); perfect information: yes; transaction costs: no; free entry/exit: yes; no influence on price (price taker); D_firm = P_market = MR; Q* where P_market = MC(Q*); P* = P_market. *[S10 slides p.44]*
- **Monopolistic competition** — Many firms; differentiated goods (fast food); perfect information: no; transaction costs: yes; free entry/exit: yes; price maker; faces its own D_firm; Q* where MR(Q*) = MC(Q*); P* = P(Q*). *[S10 slides p.44]*
- **Monopoly** — One firm (e.g., rural gas station); price maker; D_firm = D_market; Q* where MR(Q*) = MC(Q*); P* = P(Q*). Information, transaction costs, entry: N/A in the professor's table. *[S10 slides p.44]*
- **Price taker vs. price maker** — A price taker has no influence on price (perfect competition); a price maker sets price from its downward-sloping demand (monopoly, monopolistic competition). *[S10 slides p.44]*

**Formulas**

- Marginal revenue: `MR = \frac{dR}{dQ},\quad P = a - bQ \Rightarrow R = aQ - bQ^2,\; MR = a - 2bQ` *[S10 slides pp.17-19]*
- Profit max (price maker): `MR(Q^*) = MC(Q^*),\quad P^* = P(Q^*)` *[S10 slides p.44]*
- Profit max (price taker): `P_{market} = MC(Q^*)` *[S10 slides p.44]*
- Profit: `\pi = R - C = P\,Q - C(Q)` *[S10 slides p.46]*

**Worked examples**

- **Advertising spend** — Ads 0-10; TR 0, 90, 170, 240, 300, 350, 390, 420, 440, 450, 450; TC 0, 10, 30, 60, 100, 150, 210, 280, 360, 450, 550 → MB falls 90→0, MC rises 10→100; MB = MC = 50 at 5 ads; profit peaks at 200 (4 or 5 ads) *[S10 slides p.15]*
- **MR from inverse demand** — P = 10 − Q → R = 10Q − Q², MR = 10 − 2Q; revenue peaks at Q = 5 (R = 25) where MR = 0 *[S10 slides pp.17-19]*
- **Price taker at $40** — C(Q) = 125 + 4Q², P = $40 → MR = P = 40, MC = 8Q → Q* = 5; C = 225; R = 200; profit = −25. Operate in the short run, exit in the long run. *[S10 slides p.46]*
- **Monopoly** — C(Q) = 125 + 4Q², P = 100 − Q → MR = 100 − 2Q = 8Q → Q* = 10; P* = 90; C = 525; R = 900; profit = 375. Will not face entry unless patents/entry barriers are eliminated. *[S10 slides p.47]*
- **Monopolistic competition** — Same cost; P = 100 − Q for your brand → Short run identical to monopoly: Q = 10, P = 90, profit 375 (ATC 52.5). Long run: imitators enter, demand shifts to P = 50 − Q; tangency at Q = 5, P = ATC = 45, profit = 0. *[S10 slides pp.48-49]*
- **CQ5 monopoly** — P = 120 − 2Q; C = 500 + 2Q² → MR = 120 − 4Q = MC = 4Q → Q = 15; P = 90; profit = 400 *[CQ5 Q6-Q7]*
- **CQ5 price taker** — C = 50 + 10Q + 2Q²; P = 70 → 70 = 10 + 4Q → Q = 15 *[CQ5 Q8]*
- **Sandwich shop MB vs MC** — 3 workers 60 sandwiches → 4th worker 75; $5 each; wage $50 → MR = 15 × $5 = $75 > $50 → hire *[CQ5 Q10]*
- **Provo Peaks Root Beer (midterm practice)** — 350 obs, 7 states; Q (cases) on price ($) and income ($000/capita) → Q = 200.152 − 5.849P + 7.804I; price t −2.151 p 0.032 (significant); income t 6.677 p 0.000 (significant, normal good); R² 0.122 (only 12.2% of variation explained); F 24.065 *[S10 slides pp.10-11]*

## S11 — Advanced Pricing Strategies (Unique Value to Offer)

**Learning objectives**

- `S11-LO1` Know and understand the following economic terms: block pricing, commodity bundling, cross-subsidies, price discrimination, peak-load pricing, two-part pricing, transfer pricing *[course-data.js Session 11]*
- `S11-LO2` Know and understand the three forms of price discrimination *[course-data.js Session 11]*
- `S11-LO3` Know how to calculate the profit-maximizing price when using two-part pricing, block pricing, commodity bundling, peak-load pricing, cross-subsidies, and transfer pricing *[course-data.js Session 11]*
- `S11-LO4` Be able to articulate the benefits and risks of charging different prices to different customers *[course-data.js Session 11]*

**Key terms**

- **Price discrimination** — Charging different prices to different consumers (or for different units) for the same good, where the price differences are not due to cost differences. *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
- **First-degree (perfect) price discrimination** — Charging each consumer the maximum they are willing to pay for each unit; the firm captures all consumer surplus. *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
- **Second-degree price discrimination** — Charging a per-unit price that depends on how many units are bought (e.g., quantity discounts); consumers self-select. *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
- **Third-degree price discrimination** — Charging different groups (with different, identifiable elasticities) different prices, e.g., student or senior discounts. Charge the higher price to the less elastic group. *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
- **Two-part pricing** — A fixed fee for the right to buy plus a per-unit price. Profit-maximizing: set per-unit price = MC and the fixed fee = the consumer surplus each consumer receives at that price. *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
- **Block pricing** — Selling identical units only in a package (block); the profit-maximizing package price equals the total value (area under demand) of the package quantity, where package quantity is where P = MC. *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
- **Commodity bundling** — Selling two or more different products together for a single price; profitable when consumers' reservation prices are negatively correlated and the firm cannot price discriminate. *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
- **Peak-load pricing** — Charging a higher price during peak demand (when capacity is constrained and MC is high) and a lower price off-peak. Set P where MR = MC in each period. *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
- **Cross-subsidies** — Pricing one product below cost (or low) to boost demand for a complementary product that earns the profit (e.g., cheap printers, profitable ink). *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
- **Transfer pricing** — Setting the internal price at which an upstream division sells an input to a downstream division of the same firm; to maximize overall firm profit (no external market), set the transfer price equal to the upstream division's marginal cost. *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*

**Formulas**

- Markup rule: `P = \left[\frac{E}{1+E}\right] MC \quad (E = OPE < -1)` *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
- Third-degree rule: `MR_1 = MR_2 = MC \;\Rightarrow\; P_1\frac{1+E_1}{E_1} = P_2\frac{1+E_2}{E_2}` *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
- Two-part tariff: `P = MC,\quad \text{Fee} = CS = \tfrac12 (P_{choke} - MC)\,Q(MC)` *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
- Block price: `\text{Package price} = MC\cdot Q + CS \text{ at } P=MC` *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
- Transfer price (no external market): `P_T = MC_{upstream}` *[GENERAL ECON (B&P Ch 11)]*
