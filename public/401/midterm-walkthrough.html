§1 · Session 1What is strategy

Key question: Can Southwest move toward differentiation without destroying its low-cost advantage?

The one-sentence definition: strategy is a set of choices about where to compete and how to win that lets a firm create more value than rivals and keep some of it. Competitive advantage means the firm's gap between what customers will pay and what it costs to serve them is wider than rivals' gaps.

§1

The value stick

Read the stick from top to bottom. The top is the most a customer would pay (WTP). The bottom is the least a supplier would accept to provide its inputs (WTS). Everything between them is the total value created, and two lines in the middle (price and cost) cut that total into three slices.

  • Value is created only at the ends. Push WTP up (make the product better in a way customers care about) or push cost and WTS down (make it cheaper to produce, or make suppliers willing to sell for less). Nothing else changes the total.
  • Price only divides value. Moving the price line shifts value between customers and the firm. The total length of the stick stays the same.
  • Creating and capturing are different problems. A firm can lengthen the stick a lot and still keep a thin slice if buyers or suppliers are powerful. Five forces (§4) explains who captures what.
  • This stick is the backbone of the course. Cost advantage (§7) lowers the floor. Differentiation (§8) raises the ceiling. Dual advantage (§9) does both.
Exam trap"A price cut creates value for customers." It moves value to customers but creates none: buyer surplus rises by exactly what firm margin loses. Only WTP or WTS/cost moves change the total.
Check yourself: WTP $80, price $60, cost $45, WTS $30. Give all four numbers.

Buyer surplus 80 − 60 = $20. Firm margin 60 − 45 = $15. Supplier surplus 45 − 30 = $15. Total 80 − 30 = $50, and 20 + 15 + 15 = 50 confirms it.

§1

Porter's generic strategies

Porter says a firm answers two questions. What type of advantage? Either lower cost than rivals at acceptable quality, or a product customers will pay a premium for. How broad a scope? The whole industry, or one narrow segment.

  • Cost leadership wins by having the lowest cost, so it earns a margin at prices rivals can't match. It needs efficient scale, tight cost control, a standardized offering. It fails when rivals imitate, technology shifts, the firm cuts something customers were actually paying for, or costs creep back in.
  • Differentiation wins when the premium customers pay is bigger than the cost of earning it. It needs brand, design, quality, service or innovation that buyers value. It fails when the premium gets too big, buyers stop caring, or rivals copy.
  • Focus (either flavor) wins by serving one segment better or cheaper than broad players can. It needs deep segment knowledge and activities tailored to it. It fails when the segment disappears or broad rivals learn to serve it.
  • Stuck in the middle: chasing every position at once and ending up with neither the lowest cost nor a premium. Hold onto this idea for §9, where dual advantage is the exception.
Exam trapScope is about the breadth of the market served, not company size. A huge firm can be focused; a premium price to a niche = focused differentiation, not differentiation.
Check yourself: A discount airline flies only between three small regional cities at the lowest fares. Which box?

Focused low cost: cost advantage, narrow scope.

§1

Trade-offs and activity systems (fit)

  • Operational effectiveness (OE) is not strategy. Doing the same activities better than rivals is necessary, but best practices get copied fast, so OE alone gives no lasting edge.
  • Strategy requires trade-offs. Choosing what not to do is what makes a position hard to copy. A rival who tries to match you must give up something of its own.
  • Fit. Advantage sits in a system of activities that reinforce each other. Walmart's system reinforces cost leadership. Louis Vuitton's (no discounting, limited availability, company-owned boutiques, heritage production) reinforces differentiation.
  • A rival can copy one activity. Copying a whole system of interlocking choices is far harder.
Case: SouthwestOne aircraft type, point-to-point routes, fast turnarounds, no assigned seats or frills. Adding assigned seating and premium services may raise WTP, but each addition reintroduces costs and complexity the original model deleted. The case question: can you move along one dimension (differentiation) without unraveling the system that produced advantage on the other (cost)?
Check yourself: What must a rival give up to copy Southwest?

A legacy carrier would have to give up its hub-and-spoke network, its mixed fleet, and the assigned seating and premium services its business travelers pay for. Copying means dismantling its own system and revenue model. That is what a trade-off protects.

§2 · Session 2Structured problem solving

Key questions: What is theory? How do I organize a compelling case?

§2

What is theory?

A theory is a statement of what causes what, why, and under what conditions. It links an outcome (the dependent variable) to its drivers (independent variables). Good theory lets you predict what happens in a situation you have not seen before.

That's why the course keeps calling frameworks theories: five forces predicts industry profitability; VRIO predicts whether an advantage will last. When you use a framework on the exam, you're applying a cause-and-effect claim, not filling in a checklist.

Check yourself: In VRIO, what's the dependent variable?

Whether a resource gives a sustained competitive advantage (or parity, temporary, etc.). The four tests are the drivers.

§2

The Pyramid Principle, the hourglass, and SCQA

Minto's insight: clear writing comes from clear thinking. Readers absorb ideas best as a pyramid: one governing thought on top, a few grouped, logically ordered arguments under it. Lead with the conclusion, then give the support.

SCQA is how the introduction gets you to the Answer:

  • Situation: what the reader already accepts. No tension yet. "Cartier sells through its own boutiques and select jewelers."
  • Complication: what changed or went wrong. It creates the need to act. "A discount retailer offers to carry the full line, nearly doubling volume."
  • Question: the question the complication provokes. Explicit and narrow, never just "What should we do?" "Should Cartier sell through the discount channel?"
  • Answer: one complete, declarative sentence that answers the Question and could be proven false. "Cartier should decline because the channel would cost more in brand value than it adds in volume."

An Answer is never a topic ("the distribution decision"), a list of options, or a restatement of the Complication.

Exam trapOn MC, sort candidate Answers fast: a noun phrase = topic; a fact about what went wrong = Complication; "options include..." = list. The valid one is a falsifiable recommendation.
Check yourself: Is "Our three growth options and their trade-offs" a valid Answer?

No. It's a topic (and a list). A valid Answer would be "Enter the Mexican market through a local partner in 2027."

§2

Deductive and inductive support

  • Deductive: a chain (major premise, minor premise, therefore). If the premises hold, the conclusion is forced. Weakness: one rejected link kills it. Use it with tight logic and a reader willing to follow a line of reasoning.
  • Inductive: a group of parallel reasons of the same kind. Robust and easy to absorb. Weakness: reasons must be truly parallel and MECE. Use it for most recommendations and for skeptical or hurried readers.
  • Complex arguments often combine them: a deductive spine at the top, with each premise supported inductively beneath it.
Check yourself: A memo gives three independent reasons to reject an acquisition. The reader rejects one. What happens?

It weakens but survives, because inductive reasons are independent (practice Q4). If it had been a deductive chain, it would collapse.

§2

The four quality tests

  1. Single governing thought. One Answer at the apex, written as a sentence.
  2. MECE groupings. Mutually exclusive (no overlap) and collectively exhaustive (no gaps).
  3. Valid logic. Deductive chains actually follow; inductive groups are actually parallel.
  4. So what? Each group should roll up into the idea above it. If you can't say what a group adds up to, it doesn't belong.

Bonus: a strong argument also names and defeats the strongest competing answer.

Check yourself: Reasons "cuts costs," "reduces expenses," and "improves margins" support one Answer. Which test fails?

MECE (mutually exclusive). The first two overlap, and the third partly restates them.

§2

Logic trees and market sizing

  • Break the dependent variable into drivers until you reach something you can measure and act on. "Profitability" isn't actionable; "unit price" is.
  • Each level must be MECE. If the children don't sum (or multiply) to the parent, the branch is wrong.
  • Every join is an arithmetic operator. Channels add. Price and volume multiply.
  • Identity vs hypothesis. Sales = price × volume is an identity, always true. "Market share is declining" is a hypothesis about why a term moved. It must be checked against evidence before it earns a place on the page.
  • Market sizing uses the same logic: population × % who buy × frequency × volume per purchase × price.
Check yourself: A restaurant's profit fell. Build the first two levels and name one hypothesis.

Profit = Revenue − Cost. Revenue = covers (customers) × average check. Cost = fixed (rent, salaried staff) + variable (food, hourly labor). These are identities. A hypothesis: "A new competitor opened nearby and pulled covers down." It needs evidence (cover counts before/after the opening).

§3 · Session 3The macro environment: PEST

Key question: How did Khama change the PEST for De Beers?

§3

PEST and its three jobs

PEST scans forces outside the industry that no single firm controls. It does three jobs: detect the level of risk, locate bargaining power, and separate what must be accepted from what can be shaped. The analysis is the third job: deciding which factors are fixed constraints and which the firm can influence.

Case: DebswanaBotswana's president Seretse Khama and De Beers formed a joint venture to mine the country's diamonds. A firm that might have faced expropriation gained a stable long-term partner; the country gained revenue and development. Lesson: political conditions were shaped, not just accepted (job ③).
Check yourself: A manager lists a new local-content rule as a threat to live with, without asking if terms are negotiable. What job was skipped?

Job ③, separating what must be accepted from what can be shaped (practice Q5).

§3 · P

Political risk, seizure, and corruption

Political risk is the threat that political decisions or events in a country hurt the profitability of an investment. The matrix sorts risks by who they hit (macro: everyone; micro: specific firms or industries) and where they come from (bilateral: between countries; unilateral: one government acting alone).

Corruption is an exchange that influences the allocation of resources and involves using public power for private benefit. It misallocates capital, obstructs firm growth, undermines trade and investment, and distorts information.

FCPA (1977, U.S.)
  • No bribes to foreign officials to get or keep business
  • Covers U.S. companies and citizens, their subsidiaries and agents, and issuers of U.S. securities
  • DOJ enforces anti-bribery; SEC enforces accounting provisions
  • Exceptions: payments for routine government action; payments lawful under the foreign country's written law; reasonable, bona fide expenditures
UK Bribery Act (tougher)
  • Also bars private-sector bribes
  • Applies to both the payer and the receiver
Two ethical positions
  • Relativism: "When in Rome, do as the Romans do"
  • Imperialism: "My way or the highway"

Political risk can be an opportunity. Firms that add value to the state, strike alliances, and build to stay can turn government from a threat into a partner (Debswana).

Exam trapExpropriation = compensated. Confiscation = not compensated. Nationalization = a whole industry, either way. "Creeping" works through taxes and rules over time, not one act.
Check yourself: A U.S. firm pays a customs clerk a small fee to process routine paperwork it is already entitled to. FCPA violation?

Likely falls under the routine government action exception. A payment to win a contract would not.

  • GDP vs GNP: GDP is production within borders, by anyone. GNP is production by a country's own factors, anywhere.

Check yourself: Big Mac = ¥480 in Japan, £4.59 in the UK. PPP rate? If the market rate is ¥190 per £, which currency is undervalued?

480 ÷ 4.59 ≈ 104.6 yen per pound. At ¥190 per £ the pound buys more yen than PPP says, so the pound is overvalued and the yen is undervalued.

Check yourself: Classify each: a newly built house bought by a family; a Social Security check; Toyota's Kentucky plant output.

New house = I (investment). Social Security = not in G (a transfer). Toyota Kentucky = counts in U.S. GDP and in Japan's GNP.

§3 · S

Socio-cultural: Erin Meyer's Culture Map

Cultural differences are systematic and predictable. Three rules for reading the map:

  • No culture is better. Each end of a scale is a valid approach.
  • Position is relative. What matters is where two cultures sit compared with each other, not the absolute spot.
  • The same country can sit at different ends on different scales. The U.S. is low-context (very explicit) but softens negative feedback more than the Dutch or Germans.

Watch the Deciding scale: consensual cultures decide slowly but implement fast (everyone already agreed); top-down cultures decide fast and implement more slowly.

Check yourself: You negotiate with a partner from a high-context, relationship-based culture. What changes?

Invest time in the relationship before the deal (meals, personal connection). Read between the lines and watch what's not said; don't expect explicit "no." Avoid blunt public criticism. Confirm understanding in conversation rather than relying on the contract alone.

§3 · T

Technological: three infrastructures

Technological factors show where a country stands on the technology and infrastructure a business needs: equipment maturity, information systems, platforms, research funding, consumer access, roads. Assess three infrastructures and ask, for each, to what extent is it available?

  • Physical: raw materials, manufacturing equipment, transportation, energy.
  • Information: communication tech, data storage and processing, supporting laws.
  • Human: skilled, trained people. Slowest to build, so most likely the binding constraint.

A gap between infrastructures is often the opportunity: strong mobile coverage + few bank branches invites mobile banking.

§4 · Session 4Industry structure: the five forces

Key question: How did Coke structure an industry to give itself a competitive advantage?

§4

Porter's five forces

Industry structure decides how the value an industry creates is divided and how much the competitors keep. Think of the value stick: suppliers pull up on cost, buyers push down on price, substitutes cap the price, entrants add capacity and price pressure, and rivalry hands value to customers.

Price rivalry is the most destructive form. Rivalry on features, service, or brand can raise WTP and is less damaging (Coke vs Pepsi on advertising).

How to run it

  1. Define the industry: which products, which geography
  2. Identify the players in each of the five groups
  3. Assess each force's drivers; decide which are strong
  4. Explain overall profitability from the strongest forces
  5. Look for change: which forces are shifting, which can the firm influence?
  6. Position where forces are weakest, or reshape them
Common mistakes (MC favorites) 1. Wrong industry boundary (most common and costly; every force changes when it moves). 2. Treating growth as a force. A fast-growing market with weak barriers and powerful buyers draws in capacity and hands gains to customers. It turns investor capital into consumer surplus. 3. Confusing factors with forces (technology, government, complements). 4. Listing instead of concluding. 5. Treating structure as fixed.
Check yourself: Which is NOT one of the five forces: substitutes, industry growth rate, supplier power, rivalry?

Industry growth rate (practice Q6). Structure decides who keeps the value growth creates.

§4

Profit pools

The distribution of profit along a value chain rarely matches the distribution of revenue. In personal computers, PC makers competed in the largest but least profitable layer while microprocessors and software earned the margins.

  • Compare layers by profit per point of revenue = profit share ÷ revenue share.
  • Low barriers and low profit in a layer are the same fact seen twice.
  • Profit collects at the point of control. Before entering any layer, understand what protects the most profitable one.
Case: Cola WarsTwo layers, two structures. Concentrate: little capital, few competitors, strong brands → very profitable. Bottling: capital intensive, thin margins. Coke and Pepsi kept the attractive layer, set terms for bottlers through franchise agreements, and fought each other on advertising and brand more than price. They didn't just find an attractive industry; they structured one.
Check yourself: Why can two firms in the same value chain earn very different returns?

Each layer has its own five-forces structure. Concentrate faces few rivals, weak buyers (bottlers bound by franchise terms), and strong brands; bottling faces heavy capital needs, powerful retail buyers, and concentrate suppliers who set terms.

§5 · Session 5Concentration and entry

Key questions: How does concentration affect strategy? How do you break into a concentrated market?

§5

Measuring concentration: C4 and HHI

Why concentration matters: in concentrated markets firms recognize their interdependence, price competition is more restrained, and incumbents have more ability and incentive to defend. In fragmented markets no firm can influence price and rivalry is hard to contain.

  • C4 = sum of the four largest shares. Simple, but ignores how share is split among the four and ignores everyone else.
  • HHI = sum of squared shares of every firm, shares in percentage points (30% → 30² = 900). Large shares count disproportionately. Range: near 0 to 10,000.
  • Bands: below 1,000 unconcentrated, 1,000 to 1,800 moderately concentrated, above 1,800 highly concentrated.
Check yourself: Shares 35, 25, 20, 10, 10. C4 and HHI?

C4 = 35 + 25 + 20 + 10 = 90. HHI = 1,225 + 625 + 400 + 100 + 100 = 2,450 (highly concentrated).

§5

Merger review and the 2ab shortcut

  • The presumption turns on the level of concentration and the change. The merged firm's rank relative to the leader is irrelevant.
  • Pre-merger HHI being "only moderate" doesn't save a deal. The tests look at post-merger HHI and the size of the increase.
  • Market definition comes first: the same deal can look harmless or harmful depending on how the market is drawn.
  • Why two small firms merging raises no concern: 2ab is tiny when both are small (3% + 2% → 12 points). Second and third largest (25% + 20%) → 1,000 points.
Exam trapBoth tests need the change to exceed 100. And "we'd only tie the leader" is never a defense (Mini-case A).
Check yourself: Pre-merger HHI 1,500. Firms with 10% and 6% merge. Presumption?

Change = 2 × 10 × 6 = 120. Post = 1,620, below 1,800, so the concentration test fails. Merged share = 16%, below 30%, so the share test fails. No presumption (practice Q9).

Check yourself: Mini-case A in 60 seconds. Shares 25, 20, 15, 15, 10, 10, 5; a 15 merges with a 10.

Pre HHI = 625 + 400 + 225 + 225 + 100 + 100 + 25 = 1,700. Change = 300. Post = 2,000. Concentration test met (2,000 > 1,800 and 300 > 100). Share test not met (25%). Either is enough → presumption applies.

§5

Cracking a well-guarded market

Incumbents in concentrated markets are protected by scale, brands, switching costs, distribution, and the ability to retaliate. A head-on attack on their strength usually fails. Successful entrants tend to:

  • Exploit the incumbent's trade-offs. Don't ask "could they respond?" Ask "what would they have to give up to respond?" The best entry points are ones where responding would damage the incumbent's core business.
  • Start with a segment the incumbent underserves or doesn't value, then expand.
  • Change the basis of competition (business model, technology, channel) so the incumbent's assets matter less.

In-class applications: cracking internet search; whether DOJ would allow Netflix or Paramount to buy Warner Brothers.

Check yourself: A dominant search engine earns most of its revenue from ads beside results. What would it have to give up to answer an ad-free, subscription AI answer engine?

Its ad revenue on those queries. Matching the entrant cannibalizes the core business, so it is a trade-off the incumbent resists. That's the kind of entry point the framework looks for.

§6 · Session 6Theories of the firm: RBV and VRIO

Key questions: What is a firm? Why does it exist? Does AI offer a sustainable competitive advantage?

§6

Why firms exist: three theories

Transaction cost economics

Coase, Williamson. Using the market costs something: searching, negotiating, enforcing contracts. Firms exist when organizing internally is cheaper than buying. Make when transaction costs are high (specialized assets, uncertainty, hold-up risk); buy when low.

Knowledge-based view

Firms exist to create, combine, and protect knowledge markets can't easily transfer. Boundaries surround the critical knowledge.

Resource-based view

Firms are bundles of resources and capabilities. Lasting performance differences come from those bundles. The firm keeps what gives it advantage.

Check yourself: Why would a firm make something it could buy more cheaply on the market?

The sticker price ignores transaction costs. If the input needs specialized assets, faces uncertainty, or exposes the firm to hold-up (a supplier exploiting dependence), the total cost of buying is higher. Or the activity holds critical knowledge the firm wants to protect.

§6

Does industry or the firm matter more?

Studies since Rumelt (1991) find the business unit explains three to five times as much profit variance as the industry. Industry analysis (§4) tells you what the environment allows. Internal analysis (this section) tells you why one firm in that industry out-earns another.

§6

The resource-based view

RBV rests on two assumptions: resource heterogeneity (firms hold different bundles) and resource immobility (bundles can't be bought, copied, or rebuilt quickly). Where both hold, advantage can persist. Resources are stocks; capabilities are flows, the routines that put resources to work.

§6

VRIO: a gate, not a scorecard

Why resources resist imitation
  • Unique history (path dependence): built under conditions that can't be re-created
  • Causal ambiguity: even insiders can't say why it works. Cuts both ways: the firm can't reliably move it to a new plant and may destroy it in a reorganization
  • Social complexity: relationships, trust, culture
  • Legal and economic deterrents: patents, exclusive contracts, scale, network effects
Reading the verdicts
  • Parity is still required; losing it creates a disadvantage
  • Temporary earns above-normal returns until a rival buys or builds it
  • Unexploited is most actionable: the constraint is internal, in management's control
  • Sustained ≠ permanent: patents expire, substitutes arrive
  • An unanswered test is not a failed test; if O is open, no verdict yet

VRIN is the earlier version: valuable, rare, inimitable, non-substitutable. VRIO folds substitution into imitability and adds organization.

Applying it to AI: a widely available AI model is valuable but not rare, so by itself it yields parity. Any advantage comes from what's rare and hard to copy around it: proprietary data, complementary capabilities, an organization built to use it.

Common mistakesConfusing valuable with popular. Calling something rare because it's impressive. Treating expensive as inimitable (if a well-funded rival can buy it, it fails I). Forgetting organization. Auditing everything the firm owns instead of the 2 or 3 resources that explain its position.
Check yourself: Valuable, rare, organized, but a rival could buy the same equipment on the open market. Verdict?

Temporary competitive advantage. Fails imitability (practice Q10).

Check yourself: Every bank has a mobile app and customers won't open an account without one.

Competitive parity. Valuable but not rare: table stakes (practice Q11).

Check yourself (short answer 22): V, R, I pass, O fails. Verdict and why it's most actionable?

Unexploited advantage. The binding constraint is internal: structure, processes, and incentives are in management's control, while rarity and imitability depend on what rivals do.

§7 · Sessions 7 and 8Cost advantage

Key questions: How does a firm get a structural cost advantage (Aldi)? A scale cost advantage (Netflix)?

A cost advantage lowers the floor of the value stick. The firm delivers a comparable offering at lower cost, so it can match rivals' prices and earn more, or undercut them and still earn a margin.

§7

The value chain

A firm has no costs. Activities have costs. The value chain breaks the firm into the activities it performs so cost and value can be traced to their source.

To use it: list the activities → assign costs to each → identify each activity's cost driver → compare with rivals → find where the advantage is created. Class examples: Costco's few SKUs and low turnover, Ryanair's single aircraft type, Toyota's supplier network, Little Caesars' simple menu.

Check yourself: Is procurement primary or support?

Support. Primary = inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service.

§7

Structural cost advantage: Aldi

A structural advantage is made of absences. The firm doesn't do the same activities more cheaply; it arranged its business so many of them don't exist.

  • It doesn't depend on being the biggest.
  • It is many interlocking choices, each affordable only with the others (an activity system, §1).
  • It is protected by the rival's own economics, not secrecy. Copying would destroy the rival's revenue logic.

Conventional grocers have watched Aldi for fifty years without copying, because cutting assortment destroys the one-stop shop and going private label forfeits supplier payments.

§7

Economies of scale: v + F/n, MES, Netflix

Economies of scale = lower cost per unit as output per period rises, because fixed cost F is spread over more units n. Unit cost falls toward v but never below it.

  • Sources: technical (automation, bigger equipment), managerial (specialization), financial and marketing (cheaper capital, ads spread over more units), purchasing.
  • Minimum efficient scale (MES): smallest volume where unit cost is near its minimum. Efficient plants ≈ 100 ÷ MES share of demand. The cost penalty is how much it costs to run below MES, and only firms below MES pay it. Tiny MES → nearly everyone reaches it → fragmented industry.
  • Diseconomies set in when coordination, bureaucracy, and complexity raise unit cost.
  • Size is not scale. The test is whether average cost falls as volume grows. Ask at what unit cost is incurred.
  • Scale is not a network effect. Scale is supply-side (cost per unit falls). A network effect is demand-side (each user makes the product worth more to others).
Case: NetflixEach pivot (store → DVD by mail → streaming) moved cost from variable to fixed and steepened the curve. A $17B content budget at ~$12/month: 17B ÷ (12 × 12 = $144/yr) ≈ 118 million subscribers to break even on content alone. Only Netflix clears it. But scale doesn't say who keeps the gains: Starz raised its license fee tenfold in three years. Originals solved that by owning the input. Habit: locate the fixed costs, find the unit of scale, measure the slope, ask who appropriates the gains.
Check yourself: F = $600,000, v = $4, n = 300,000. Unit cost? And MES = 5% of demand: how many efficient plants?

4 + 600,000 ÷ 300,000 = $6 (Q12). 100 ÷ 5 = 20 plants (Q15).

Check yourself (short answer 21): Why didn't Blockbuster's 10× size give it a scale advantage?

Scale economies exist only when average cost falls with volume. Blockbuster incurred cost at the store level (lease, labor, local inventory replicated per location), so cost per rental was flat however big it got. Netflix incurred fixed costs once for the nation and spread them over every subscriber. Blockbuster had size without scale; Netflix had scale before size.

§7

Economies of scope

Scope = lower average cost from producing a wider range of products, by sharing resources across them: R&D, brand, distribution, customer accounts, fulfillment. Amazon shares technology, fulfillment centers, and Prime across categories. Scale = more of the same thing; scope = more different things on the same base.

§7

Learning and experience curves (Wright's law)

  • Learning curve: labor cost per unit falls as workers repeat a task. Experience curve: all costs fall with cumulative volume, as learning gets built into people, process, tooling, and design.
  • Wright's law (1936): each doubling of cumulative output cuts unit cost by a constant percentage.
  • Early doublings come fast; later ones take years, so curves flatten as industries mature.
  • A follower can't catch up by building a big factory. It has to live through the cumulative volume, and the leader keeps producing. Rivian needs 4.7 doublings to reach Tesla's cumulative volume.
  • Erodes when learning leaks (employees, suppliers), the curve flattens, or new technology resets the axis.
Exam trapCount doublings, not units. 1,000th → 4,000th is two doublings (×0.8 × 0.8), not three or four.
Check yourself: 80% curve, 1,000th unit costs $50. Cost of the 4,000th?

Two doublings: 50 × 0.8 × 0.8 = $32 (Q13).

§7

Stacking and maintaining cost advantages

  • The strongest positions combine sources that feed each other. Aldi's limited assortment (structural) creates purchasing scale at the item level.
  • A cost gap creates a choice: harvest it as margin or reinvest it in lower prices to build share.
  • Each type needs its own maintenance: structure needs discipline against added complexity; scale needs share defense; experience needs continued motion down the curve.
Check yourself: Why can a small firm have a cost advantage over a much bigger rival?

Its advantage may be structural (a design that deletes costs at any size, like Aldi) or experience-based, neither of which needs current size. And the big rival may have size without scale (Blockbuster) or suffer diseconomies.

§8 · Sessions 9 and 10Differentiation

Key questions: Is what the company sees as added value recognized and rewarded by customers (Chipotle)? Is differentiation beyond the product better or worse than product differentiation (A More Co)?

§8

The economics: raise WTP by more than cost

Differentiation raises the ceiling of the value stick: raising WTP by more than it costs to create the reason buyers pay a premium. Being unique or famous is not the test. The customer must recognize the difference and reward it.

  • The test: compare ΔWTP with Δunit cost, in dollars. If cost rises more than WTP, the move destroys value.
  • Consumer surplus = WTP − price. Success = customers better off and the firm with a higher margin.
  • Creating vs capturing: recompute buyer surplus, firm margin, and supplier surplus before and after. Powerful buyers who can verify quality themselves can take most of what's created.
  • The diagnostic: if profits improved but no price moved, the achievement was in cost, not differentiation. Cost achievements diffuse to rivals.
CasesChipotle: the test isn't what the company believes adds value; it's whether customers recognize it and pay for it. Ferrari: enormous costs, tiny scale, and the industry's highest margins anyway, because the wedge between WTP and cost is so wide. Volume is capped below demand on purpose.
Check yourself: A redesign raises WTP by $4 and unit cost by $5.

It destroys $1 of value per unit (Q16), however "better" the product is.

Check yourself: Customer satisfaction rose, but prices and volume didn't. Did it differentiate?

No evidence of it. Differentiation shows up as customers rewarding the difference (a premium or more volume). Unrewarded satisfaction is just cost.

§8

Price discrimination: three degrees

Price discrimination captures more of the buyer surplus by charging according to WTP. 1st: each customer pays his or her reservation price (auctions). 2nd: price varies with quantity purchased (bulk discounts). 3rd: price varies by customer group (student and senior discounts; business and economy fares).

Check yourself: A "buy 2, get 1 free" deal is which degree?

Second degree: price depends on quantity, not on who you are.

§8

How buyers judge quality: search, experience, credence

Sources of differentiation. Product: features, performance, quality, design, reliability. Beyond the product: service, convenience, customization, brand and reputation, experience, mission and values, relationships, community.

Product differences are easier for customers to see and for rivals to copy. Differences rooted in brand, relationships, and experience are harder to demonstrate and harder to imitate. That's the A More Co trade-off.

Check yourself: Best example of a credence good: laptop, restaurant meal, haircut, daily supplement with long-term claims?

The supplement (Q17). You can't verify it even after use, so it must be signaled.

§8

Strategy canvas

Plot the factors an industry competes on against the level each competitor offers. A good strategy shows focus (peaks and valleys, not a flat line) and divergence from rivals. If your curve sits on top of a rival's, you're competing on the same terms.

§8

Four actions framework

EliminateWhich factors the industry takes for granted should go?Cirque: animal shows, star performers
RaiseWhich should rise well above the industry standard?Cirque: venue quality
ReduceWhich should fall well below the industry standard?Cirque: humor and thrills
CreateWhich should be offered for the first time?Cirque: artistic theme, refined environment

Left column (eliminate, reduce) cuts cost. Right column (raise, create) raises WTP. Done together, the value curve diverges from rivals on the canvas above. Other examples: Nintendo Wii and Airbnb. This is also a route to dual advantage (§9).

Check yourself: Map each ERRC action onto the strategy canvas above.

Eliminate: Cirque's dots drop to the floor (animals, stars). Reduce: lower than circus (humor/thrills). Raise: above circus (venue). Create: factors where the circus is near zero and Cirque is high (theme, refined environment).

§8

Jobs to be done, the consumption chain, segmentation

  • Jobs to be done: customers hire a product to do a job. Define the job, not the product category, and competitors and opportunities look different.
  • Mapping the consumption chain: trace every step of the customer's experience (above) and look for a step to differentiate on.
  • Customer segmentation: group customers by similar needs; look for segments overserved (paying for more than they want → cost play) or underserved (needs unmet → differentiation play).

§9 · Session 11Dual advantage

Key question: How can a firm have both low cost and differentiation? What share of Costco's advantage is cost vs differentiation?

§9

Dual advantage on the value stick

Generic strategies (§1) warn that pursuing both cost and differentiation leaves a firm stuck in the middle. Dual advantage is the exception: lower costs and higher WTP at the same time.

  • On the value stick it moves the floor down and the ceiling up at once. It's an extreme on both ends, not a moderate position.
  • What makes it real is a reinforcing loop: each element makes the next one cheaper or more valuable.
  • Dual vs stuck: a stuck firm borrows activities from two systems that conflict. A dual-advantage firm builds one system whose parts feed each other.

How firms get there: choices that cut cost and raise value together (a curated assortment is cheaper and easier to shop); scale or experience funding quality; eliminating what customers don't value and reinvesting in what they do (four actions); a revenue model that changes the trade-off (membership fees let a retailer price near cost).

§9

Case: Costco's reinforcing loop

Costco carries roughly 4,000 items, caps markups near 14%, and earns most of its operating profit from membership fees. Limited selection lowers cost and concentrates buying power. Low prices and curated quality raise what members will pay to belong. Membership income funds lower prices, which attracts more members. Sam's Club runs the same warehouse model, so the comparison isolates what Costco's loop adds.

Why it's hard to attack: the strongest positions stack sources that reinforce one another. Ask of any strong competitor how its sources interact, because that interaction is what a challenger must break.

Check yourself: Two warehouse retailers both have low costs and loyal customers. In one, selection → lower cost → lower prices → more members → buying power. What does it have that the other lacks?

A reinforcing loop (Q18). That separates dual advantage from doing two things well.

Check yourself: How would you estimate how much of Costco's edge is cost vs WTP?

Benchmark against Sam's Club. Cost side: compare operating cost as a share of sales and prices on matched items. WTP side: compare membership fee levels, renewal rates, and sales per member/visit at similar prices. The fee premium members pay and renew at is a direct read on WTP.
